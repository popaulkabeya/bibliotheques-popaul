# Foreword

In a small town in any country of the world, there is a street where a church, a mosque, perhaps a synagogue, a Hindu temple or a pagoda stand side by side. Those who enter them do not pray in the same language, do not read the same book, do not count the feast days by the same calendar. And yet, if each of them stepped out onto the threshold to say aloud what their book asks them to do toward their neighbor, one would be struck to hear, beneath different words, almost the same sentence.

This book began with that intuition, and it set out to verify it. Not by summarizing what “all religions say,” a convenient formula that often ends up saying nothing, but by going to look in the Scriptures themselves for the passages where the world’s five great religious traditions meet.

## The Method Followed

**The texts first.** Each chapter rests on quotations, and each quotation was reread in its source before being retained. This book does not report what the Bible, the Qur’an, the Upanishads or the Dhammapada are said to affirm: it quotes them. The reader may turn to each reference.

**Old translations.** The translations retained are those in the public domain, the same ones found in this library: Louis Segond for the Christian Bible, Kazimirski for the Qur’an, Burnouf for the Bhagavad-Gita. Their French is sometimes a century and a half old. It has been kept as it is, because a retouched quotation is no longer a quotation. Where no free French translation existed, the passage was translated for this book, and the reference bears the note *working translation*.

**A chronological order.** In each chapter, the five traditions always speak in the same order: **Hinduism**, whose Vedas are the oldest scriptures of the five; **Judaism**, whose Torah is the common root of the three Abrahamic religions; **Buddhism**, born in India in the 5th century BCE; **Christianity**, which appeared in the 1st century; **Islam**, in the 7th. This order is not a ranking: it simply follows the thread of time.

**Where they converge, and where they differ.** Each theme ends with a summary box. It states what the texts truly have in common, and then, honestly, where they do not say the same thing. For to bring together is not to confuse. A Buddhist does not pray to a creator God; a Muslim does not hold Jesus to be the Son of God; a Hindu believes he will return in another life, a Jew or a Christian does not. To erase these differences would be to fail in respect toward each of them. The last part of the book is devoted entirely to them.

## How to Read This Book

Part One presents the five traditions and their Scriptures: what the Torah is, what the Tipitaka contains, how the Qur’an was formed. It reads like a map before the journey.

The five following parts explore seventeen themes, grouped by broad domains: the Principle (God, the Absolute, light), the relationship to others (the Golden Rule, compassion, peace, forgiveness, giving, family), conduct (truth, honesty, self-mastery, humility), the inner life (prayer, the heart, detachment) and destiny (deeds and their fruits, death and what follows it). Each theme fits in a few pages and can be read on its own.

Part Seven states what sets the five traditions apart; the eighth concludes. In the appendix, a table gathers the five formulations of the Golden Rule, followed by the list of sources.

# Part One — The Five Traditions and Their Scriptures

<p class="part-intro">Before comparing, one must know what one is talking about. Each tradition has its own way of receiving, writing down and transmitting its sacred texts — and it is not the same everywhere.</p>

## Hinduism: The Vedas and the Bhagavad-Gita

Hinduism has no founder, no date of birth, no single book. Its faithful readily call it *sanatana dharma*, “the eternal order.” It was born of the religion of the Indo-Aryan-speaking peoples settled in northern India in the 2nd millennium BCE, and it has grown richer over more than three thousand years without ever breaking with its origins.

Its scriptures fall into two great families.

**The Shruti, “that which was heard.”** These are the texts held to be revealed, which the sages of ancient times, the *rishis*, are said to have perceived rather than composed. They are first of all the four **Vedas** — the *Rig-Veda*, a collection of more than a thousand hymns, the oldest Indian religious text (around 1500-1200 BCE), then the *Sama-Veda*, the *Yajur-Veda* and the *Atharva-Veda*. To each Veda are attached ritual commentaries and, lastly, the **Upanishads** (from about the 8th century BCE onward), philosophical meditations on the Self (*atman*) and the Absolute (*Brahman*). They are also called *Vedanta*, “the end of the Vedas.”

**The Smriti, “that which is remembered.”** These are the texts of tradition: the laws (including the *Laws of Manu*), the great epics — the *Ramayana* and the *Mahabharata*, the longest poem in the world —, the *Puranas* and the manuals of the schools of yoga. It is at the heart of the *Mahabharata* that the **Bhagavad-Gita** is found, the “Song of the Blessed One,” composed between the 5th and the 2nd century BCE: on a battlefield, the god Krishna teaches the warrior Arjuna action without attachment, knowledge and devotion. It is doubtless the most widely read text of Hinduism.

The sacred language is **Sanskrit**. About **1.2 billion** people identify with Hinduism today, mainly in India and Nepal.

## Judaism: The Written Torah and the Oral Torah

Judaism is the religion of the people of Israel, which traces its covenant with God back to Abraham, and the gift of the Law to Moses, on Mount Sinai.

Its Bible, the **Hebrew Bible**, is called the *Tanakh*, after the initials of its three parts:

- the **Torah** (“the teaching,” “the Law”): the five books of Moses, from Genesis to Deuteronomy;
- the **Nevi’im**, “the Prophets”: Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings, then Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the twelve minor prophets;
- the **Ketuvim**, “the Writings”: the Psalms, the Proverbs, Job, the Song of Songs, Ruth, the Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther, Daniel, Ezra-Nehemiah and the Chronicles.

Written almost entirely in **Hebrew** (a few chapters of Daniel and Ezra are in Aramaic), it took shape over nearly a millennium. Judaism counts it as twenty-four books; Christians, who received it under the name of the Old Testament, divide it differently.

Alongside the written Torah, Jewish tradition recognizes an **oral Torah**, handed down from master to disciple. It was set down in writing in the **Mishnah**, around the year 200 CE, then commented upon in the **Gemara**; Mishnah and Gemara together form the **Talmud**, of which there are two versions, that of Jerusalem and that of Babylon (5th-6th centuries). A short tractate of the Mishnah, the **Pirkei Avot** or “Sayings of the Fathers,” gathers the moral maxims of the sages; it is one of the most widely read and most often quoted Jewish texts.

Judaism has about **15 million** adherents. With its Scriptures, it is the common source of Christianity and Islam.

## Buddhism: The Tipitaka and the Dhammapada

Buddhism was born of the experience of one man: Siddhartha Gautama, a prince of the Shakya clan, in northern India, in the 5th century BCE (his exact dates are debated). Struck by suffering, old age and death, he left his palace, searched for years on end, and attained, under a tree at Bodh-Gaya, **Awakening** (*bodhi*). He then became the **Buddha**, “the Awakened One,” and taught for forty-five years.

The Buddha wrote nothing. His teaching, the *Dharma*, was recited and memorized by his disciples, then set down in writing in the 1st century BCE, in Ceylon, in the **Pali** language. This canon is called the **Tipitaka**, the “Three Baskets”:

- the **Vinaya**, the discipline of monks and nuns;
- the **Sutta**, the discourses of the Buddha, arranged in five collections;
- the **Abhidhamma**, the systematic analysis of the doctrine.

In the fifth collection of discourses is found the **Dhammapada**, “the Path of the Law”: 423 verses divided into 26 chapters, which sum up Buddhist ethics in brief maxims. It is the most widely translated Buddhist text in the world. The Buddhism of the Great Vehicle (*Mahayana*), widespread in China, Tibet, Korea and Japan, has added other scriptures in Sanskrit, Chinese and Tibetan, such as the *Lotus Sutra* or the *Heart Sutra*.

Buddhism does not rest on faith in a creator God. It offers a path of liberation from suffering, which each person is invited to verify through his own experience. It has about **320 million** adherents, mostly in Asia.

## Christianity: The Old and the New Testament

Christianity was born in the 1st century CE, in Palestine, around **Jesus of Nazareth**, whom his disciples recognized as the Messiah (in Greek *Christos*) foretold by the prophets of Israel, the Son of God who died on the cross and rose again.

The Christian Bible comprises two collections:

- the **Old Testament**, which takes up the Jewish Scriptures. Its contents vary from one Church to another: Protestants count 39 books, Catholics 46, the Orthodox Churches more still, and the Ethiopian Church up to 81 books for its Bible as a whole;
- the **New Testament**, 27 books written in **Greek** between about the years 50 and 100: the four **Gospels** (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John), which recount the life and teaching of Jesus; the **Acts of the Apostles**; the **Epistles**, letters of Paul and other apostles to the first communities; and **Revelation**.

The list of recognized books, the *canon*, was settled gradually; the oldest list identical to the present 27 books is that of Athanasius of Alexandria, in 367.

Christianity is today the most widespread religion in the world, with about **2.3 billion** adherents divided among Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants and the ancient Eastern Churches.

## Islam: The Qur’an and the Sunna

Islam was born in the 7th century in Arabia. According to Muslim tradition, the angel Gabriel (*Jibril*) transmitted the word of God to the prophet **Muhammad**, at Mecca and then at Medina, from the year 610 until his death in 632. *Islam* means “submission” or “surrender of oneself” to God; the *muslim* is the one who surrenders himself to Him.

The **Qur’an** (*al-Qur’an*, “the Recitation”) is held by Muslims to be the very word of God, revealed in **Arabic**. It has **114 suras** (chapters), of very unequal length, arranged roughly from the longest to the shortest, with the exception of the first, the *Fatiha*, “the Opening.” At first memorized and noted down on various materials, it was gathered into a single volume under the caliph Uthman, around 650. The Qur’an presents itself as the confirmation of earlier revelations: it names Abraham, Moses, David, Jesus and Mary, and calls Jews and Christians “the People of the Book.”

Alongside the Qur’an, the **Sunna**, the “tradition” of the Prophet, serves as a guide. It is known through the **hadiths**, accounts of his words and deeds, gathered in the 9th century into great collections, of which the most respected among Sunnis are those of **al-Bukhari** and **Muslim**. In the 13th century, the imam an-Nawawi drew from them forty fundamental hadiths, the *Forty Hadiths*, very widely memorized.

Islam has about **2 billion** adherents, Sunni for the great majority, Shia for about one in eight.

# Part Two — The Principle

<p class="part-intro">Where everything comes from, where everything is going. Before telling man how to live, each tradition tells him what surpasses him — and almost all of them, to say it, resort to the same image: light.</p>

## 1. The Ultimate: God, the Absolute, Nirvana

None of the five traditions makes man the measure of all things. All turn him toward a first reality, which neither is born nor dies, and which gives its meaning to the rest. But it is precisely here that the words differ most: some call it God and pray to it as a Father or a Lord; Hinduism names it Brahman and recognizes it under a thousand faces; Buddhism refuses to think of it as a creator and designates it by what it is not. Each voice must therefore be listened to attentively, without lending it the words of another.

### Hinduism

The oldest religious text of India already affirms, beneath the multitude of divine names, the unity of a single being:

> The divine spirit that moves in the sky is called Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni. The sages give the one being more than one name: it is Agni, Yama, Matarishvan.

<p class="ref">— Rig-Veda I, 164, 46 · tr. Langlois, 1848-1851</p>

The Upanishads go further: this one Reality, Brahman, is also the innermost Self of every being. The sage Uddalaka teaches it to his son:

> That subtle essence (the root of all), in it everything that exists has its Self. It is the True. It is the Self, and you, O Śvetaketu, are That.

<p class="ref">— Chandogya Upanishad VI, 8, 7 · working translation after Müller, 1879</p>

In the Bhagavad-Gita, the Absolute reveals itself as a personal God, Krishna, who shows the warrior Arjuna his “universal form”:

> There then, in the body of the God of gods, the son of Pandu saw the whole Universe, One in its multiplicity.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XI, 13 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

The Shema, which the Jew recites morning and evening, proclaims the unity of God and at once draws from it a demand for love:

> Hear, O Israel: the LORD is our God, the LORD is one! You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul and with all your might.

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 6, 4-5 · tr. Zadoc Kahn, French Rabbinate Bible, 1899-1906</p>

> Acknowledge now, and impress it upon your heart, that the LORD alone is God, in heaven above as here below on earth, and that there is no other!

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 4, 39 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Thus says the LORD, king and redeemer of Israel, the LORD-Cebaot: “I am the first, I am the last, apart from me there is no God!”

<p class="ref">— Isaiah 44, 6 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

The Buddha does not speak of a God. Yet he affirms, in one of his “solemn utterances,” that there exists a reality which escapes becoming, and that it is this which makes deliverance possible:

> “There is, O Bhikkhus, an unborn, unoriginated, uncreated, unformed. If there were not, O Bhikkhus, this unborn, unoriginated, uncreated, unformed, there would be no way out of the world of the born, the originated, the created, the formed. Since, O Bhikkhus, there is an unborn, unoriginated, uncreated, unformed, there is therefore a way out of the born, the originated, the created, the formed.”

<p class="ref">— Udana VIII, 3 · working translation after Strong, 1902</p>

This “unborn” bears a name: Nirvana, which the Dhammapada places above all the goods of this world.

> Health is the best of acquisitions; contentment, the best of riches; trust, the best of kin; Nirvana, the supreme happiness.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 204 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

Asked about the first of the commandments, Jesus answers by quoting the profession of faith of Israel:

> Jesus answered: This is the first: Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God is the one Lord; and: You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind, and with all your strength.

<p class="ref">— Mark 12, 29-30 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> God is Spirit, and those who worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.

<p class="ref">— John 4, 24 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

The first letter of John adds the definition that sums up the whole of the Christian faith:

> Beloved, let us love one another; for love is of God, and whoever loves is born of God and knows God. He who does not love has not known God, for God is love.

<p class="ref">— 1 John 4, 7-8 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The sura of Unity, one of the shortest in the Qur’an, is held by tradition to be the summary of the whole faith:

> Say: God is one.
>
> He is the God to whom all beings turn in their needs.
>
> He has not begotten, and was not begotten.
>
> He has no equal in anyone whatsoever.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 112, 1-4 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> God is the only God; there is no other God but him, the Living, the Unchanging. Neither slumber nor sleep has any hold on him. All that is in the heavens and on the earth belongs to him. […] His throne extends over the heavens and the earth, and their keeping costs him no effort. He is the Most High, the Great.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2, 255, the “Throne Verse” [Kazimirski II, 256] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Nothing resembles him; he hears and sees all.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 42, 11 [Kazimirski XLII, 9] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

The five traditions direct man toward a **single** reality, which neither is born nor dies and which escapes all representation. The Shema of Israel, taken up by Jesus, and the sura of Unity proclaim that it is one; the Rig-Veda sees “the one being” beneath all the divine names; the Buddha affirms an “unborn, uncreated.” All also say that it surpasses images: “Nothing resembles him,” says the Qur’an; “God is Spirit,” says the Gospel; Nirvana is “unformed.”

#### Where They Differ

The words part company at once. For Judaism, Christianity and Islam, this reality is a **personal God** and creator — whom Christianity confesses in three persons, which the other two reject. Hinduism thinks of it now as an impersonal Absolute, Brahman, identical with the Self of each (“you are That”), now as a God whom one loves, Krishna. Buddhism, for its part, posits **no creator God**: the “unborn” is not someone to whom one prays; it is the way out of suffering. Part Seven returns to this difference, the deepest of all.

</div>

## 2. Light

There is not one tradition that does not speak of light. The word designates in turn the divinity itself, the word that guides, the awakening of the mind, the radiance of the righteous man. It is the most universal image of the sacred — and it is this that gave this book its title.

### Hinduism

> In the highest golden sheath is Brahman, without passions and without parts. It is pure, it is the light of lights […]. There shine neither the sun, nor the moon and the stars, nor these lightnings, and still less this fire. When He shines, everything shines after him; by his light all this is illumined.

<p class="ref">— Mundaka Upanishad II, 2, 9-10 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

When Krishna unveils his divine form, the narrator finds no other comparison:

> If in the sky there rose all at once the Light of a thousand suns, it would be comparable to the splendor of that magnanimous God.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XI, 12 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Light of luminous bodies, he is beyond darkness. Knowledge, object of knowledge, goal of knowledge, he is in the depths of every heart.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XIII, 17 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

> Of David. The Lord is my light and my salvation: whom should I fear? The Lord is the rampart that protects my life: whom should I dread?

<p class="ref">— Psalm 27, 1 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Torah itself is light for the one who follows it:

> Your word is a torch that lights my steps, a light that shines upon my path.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 119, 105 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

And God has set a lamp within man:

> The soul of man is a divine torch, which carries its gleams into the recesses of the heart.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 20, 27 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> I, the LORD, have called you for righteousness and I take you by the hand; I protect you and I establish you as the federation of the peoples and the light of the nations.

<p class="ref">— Isaiah 42, 6 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

In the evening of his life, the Buddha refuses to let his disciples lean on any outside authority, even his own:

> Therefore, O Ananda, be lamps unto yourselves. Be a refuge unto yourselves. Seek no outside refuge. Hold fast to the truth as a lamp. Hold fast to the truth as a refuge. Seek refuge in no one other than yourselves.

<p class="ref">— Mahaparinibbana Sutta II, 33 · working translation after Rhys Davids, 1881</p>

> By day shines the sun; by night shines the moon; armed from head to foot, shines the warrior; in meditation, shines the Brahmana. But by day as by night, with unbroken radiance, shines the Buddha.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 387 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> He who, at first heedless, afterward ceases to be so, he spreads in this world a radiance like that of the moon freed from clouds.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 172 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

> The message we have heard from him, and which we declare to you, is that God is light, and that in him there is no darkness.

<p class="ref">— 1 John 1, 5 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Jesus spoke to them again, and said: I am the light of the world; he who follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life.

<p class="ref">— John 8, 12 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

And the light received must in turn enlighten others:

> You are the light of the world. A city set on a mountain cannot be hidden; and one does not light a lamp to put it under the bushel, but one puts it on the lampstand, and it gives light to all who are in the house. Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5, 14-16 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The “Verse of Light” is one of the most meditated-upon texts of Muslim mysticism:

> God is the LIGHT of the heavens and of the earth. This light is like a niche in which there is a lamp, a lamp placed in a crystal, a crystal like a brilliant star; this lamp is lit with the oil of a blessed tree, an olive tree that is neither of the East nor of the West, and whose oil shines even though fire does not touch it. It is light upon light. God leads to his light whom he will, and sets forth parables for men; for he knows all things.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 24, 35 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Light has come to you from God, as well as this clear Book by which God will guide those who follow his will into the paths of salvation. He will bring them out of darkness into light by his will, and will direct them on the straight path.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 5, 15-16 [Kazimirski V, 18] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Everywhere, the Ultimate is light, and a light **beyond all lights**: “there shine neither the sun nor the moon,” say the Upanishads; “God is light, and in him there is no darkness,” says the apostle John; “God is the light of the heavens and of the earth,” says the Qur’an. Everywhere too, this light is communicated to man: it is “in the depths of every heart” in the Gita, the disciples of Jesus become “the light of the world,” he who awakens “spreads a radiance like that of the moon” in the Dhammapada.

#### Where They Differ

Where does the light come from? For the religions born of Abraham, it comes from God and guides through revelation: the Law, the Gospel, the “clear Book.” Christianity goes further: Jesus identifies himself with it in person. Hinduism discovers it within, in the Self. Buddhism refuses any outside light: “be lamps unto yourselves.”

# Part Three — The Other

<p class="part-intro">It is here that the five voices come closest together. Who is my neighbor, what do I owe him, how am I to answer the harm he does me: on these questions, texts separated by centuries and by thousands of kilometers sometimes seem to answer one another word for word.</p>

## 3. The Golden Rule

The “Golden Rule” is the name given to the principle that asks us to treat others as we would wish to be treated ourselves. It is found in two forms: one negative (“do not do to others what you would not want done to you”), the other positive (“do for others what you would want done for you”). No other moral maxim is so widely shared: all five traditions know it, and several present it as the summary of their whole Law.

### Hinduism

In the thirteenth book of the Mahabharata, the great epic of India, the Golden Rule is given as the summary of *dharma*, the just order:

> He who regards all creatures as himself, and behaves toward them as toward himself […] attains happiness. […] Let one never do to another what one holds to be harmful to oneself. That, in brief, is the rule of Righteousness. […] When one harms another, the one who has suffered the wrong turns and harms in his turn; likewise, when one cherishes another, the other cherishes in his turn. Let one govern one’s conduct accordingly.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata XIII (Anushasana-parva), section 113 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

### Judaism

It is in the Torah that the oldest written formulation of this commandment is found, the one that will become the “second” commandment of Jesus:

> You shall not take vengeance nor bear a grudge against the children of your people, but love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19, 18 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

A few verses further on, the same chapter extends this “as yourself” to the stranger:

> He shall be to you as one of your countrymen, the stranger who sojourns with you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt I am the LORD your God.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19, 34 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

In the 1st century BCE, the sage Hillel made it the summary of the whole Law, in a story that has remained famous:

> Once a Gentile came to Shammai, and said: “Make me a proselyte, but on condition that you teach me the whole law, yes, the whole of it, while I stand on one leg.” Shammai drove him away with the builder’s rule he held in his hand. When he came to Hillel with the same challenge, Hillel converted him by answering him on the spot: “What is hateful to yourself, do not do to your neighbor. This is the whole law, and the rest is its commentary” […].

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a · after Hershon, *A Talmudic Miscellany*, 1880, tr. by the library. The Aramaic adds: “go, study.”</p>
### Buddhism

The Dhammapada grounds the prohibition against doing harm on a simple turning back upon oneself: everyone fears for his own life, therefore everyone must spare the lives of others.

> Everyone trembles before violence, everyone trembles before death. Let each do what he would wish another to do; let him not kill; let him not cause to kill.
>
> Everyone trembles before violence; to everyone life is dear. Let each do what he would wish another to do; let him not kill, let him not cause to kill.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 129-130 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

The phrase “let each do what he would wish another to do” is an interpretation by the French translator; Max Müller’s English translation (1881) renders the same passage as “remember that you are like unto them.” The text thus first invites us to recognize ourselves in the other, and from that draws the prohibition against doing harm. A parallel collection, the Udanavarga, states the rule explicitly:

> Wherever one looks, there is nothing dearer to a man than himself; therefore, since it is the same thing that is dear to you and dear to others, do not wound others with what makes you suffer yourself.

<p class="ref">— Udanavarga V, 18 · working translation after Rockhill, 1883</p>

### Christianity

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus gives the Golden Rule in its positive form, and makes it the summary of all Scripture:

> All things whatsoever you would that men should do to you, do you even so to them, for this is the law and the prophets.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 7, 12 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

The Gospel of Luke places it at the heart of the teaching on love of enemies (Luke 6, 31), which extends it beyond simple reciprocity.

### Islam

The Golden Rule of Islam is formulated in a hadith that the imam an-Nawawi kept among the forty fundamental sayings of the Prophet:

> “None of you [truly] believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 13) and Muslim (no. 45) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

In one of the versions reported by Muslim, the transmitter hesitates between two words, and it is the second that comes first:

> “By Him who holds my soul in His hand, no servant [truly] believes until he loves for his neighbor — or he said: for his brother — what he loves for himself.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by Muslim (no. 45) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

#### Where They Converge

This is the clearest convergence in the whole of this book. All five traditions know the Golden Rule, and several make it the **summary of their Law**: “this is the law and the prophets,” says Jesus; “this, in brief, is the rule of Righteousness,” says the Mahabharata; “none of you truly believes” without it, says the hadith. The Torah had laid it down first: “love your neighbor as yourself” (Leviticus 19, 18); the sage Hillel gave it as “the whole law,” of which “the rest is commentary.” Everywhere, it rests on the same gesture: **putting oneself in the place of the other**.

#### Where They Differ

The form varies: negative (“do not do to others…”) in the Mahabharata and the Udanavarga, positive (“do to them…”) in the Gospel and the hadith. The foundation varies as well: the identity of one same Self in all beings for Hinduism, the shared experience of fear and suffering for Buddhism, the love of God and neighbor for Judaism and Christianity, faith for Islam. And the circle does not everywhere have the same extent: “all creatures” in India, the “neighbor” in the Bible, the “brother” or the “neighbor” in the hadith.

</div>

## 4. Compassion and Mercy

The Golden Rule says what is just; compassion goes further. It is that movement of the heart which makes one suffer from the suffering of another and drives one to relieve it. The Abrahamic religions contemplate it first in God, “the Merciful,” and ask man to resemble Him. The traditions of India make it a disposition to be cultivated toward all living beings.

### Hinduism

The Bhagavad-Gita draws the portrait of the man whom God loves:

> The man without hatred for any living thing, kind and merciful, without selfishness, without self-regard, equal in pleasure and in pain, patient; Joyful, always in a state of Union, master of himself, firm in good purpose, his mind and reason fixed on me, my servant: that man is dear to me.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XII, 13-14 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

The Upanishads give the deep reason for this benevolence: he who sees the same Self in all can no longer hate anything.

> And he who beholds all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, never turns away from it. When, for the man who understands, the Self has become all things, what sorrow, what trouble can there be for him who has once beheld that oneness?

<p class="ref">— Isha Upanishad 6-7 · working translation after Müller, 1879</p>

### Judaism

When God reveals Himself to Moses on Sinai, it is first His mercy that He proclaims:

> The Divinity passed before him and proclaimed: “ADONAI is the eternal Being, all-powerful, gracious, merciful, slow to anger, full of benevolence and equity; He keeps His favor to the thousandth generation; He bears with crime, rebellion, fault, but He does not absolve them […].”

<p class="ref">— Exodus 34, 6-7 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Gracious and merciful is the LORD, slow to anger and abounding in grace. The LORD is good to all, His pity extends to all His creatures.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 145, 8-9 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud dares a striking image: God Himself prays that His mercy may prevail over His rigor.

> Thus does He pray: “May it please Me that My mercy prevail over My anger, that all My attributes be clothed in compassion, and that I deal with My children according to the attribute of goodness, and that for their sake I pass beyond judgment.”

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 7a · after Hershon, 1880, library translation</p>

### Buddhism

The Metta Sutta, one of the most recited texts of early Buddhism, describes boundless benevolence through the simplest image there is:

> As a mother, at the risk of her life, watches over her own child, her only child, so let each cultivate toward all beings a boundless (friendly) mind. And let him cultivate benevolence toward the whole world, a boundless (friendly) mind, above, below and across, without obstacle, without hatred, without enmity. Standing, walking, sitting or lying down, as long as he is awake, let him devote himself to this state of mind; this (way of) living, it is said, is the best in this world.

<p class="ref">— Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipata 148-150 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

> It is by gentleness that anger must be conquered; by good that evil must be conquered; by liberality, avarice; by truth, falsehood.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 223 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

> Jesus answered him: You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind. This is the first and greatest commandment. And here is the second, which is like it: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 22, 37-40 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Be therefore merciful, as your Father is merciful.

<p class="ref">— Luke 6, 36 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

To the question “who is my neighbor?”, Jesus answers with the parable of the Good Samaritan, in which the one who helps the wounded man is precisely a despised foreigner:

> But a Samaritan, who was traveling, having come there, was moved with compassion when he saw him. He went to him, and bound up his wounds, pouring oil and wine on them […]. Which of these three seems to you to have been the neighbor of the one who fell among the robbers? It is the one who showed mercy to him, answered the doctor of the law. And Jesus said to him: Go, and you, do likewise.

<p class="ref">— Luke 10, 33-37 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

All the suras of the Qur'an, save one, open with the same formula, which names nothing of God but His mercy:

> *In the name of the gracious and merciful God*.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 1, 1, the basmala · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> […] my mercy embraces all things; I destine it for those who fear, who give alms and who believe in my signs […].

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 7, 156 [Kazimirski VII, 155] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

> We have sent you, *O Muhammad!* only as a mercy for the universe.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 21, 107 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

And the kindness owed to men extends in circles, from parents to the stranger:

> Worship God and associate nothing with Him. Show kindness to your fathers and mothers, to your kin, to orphans, to the poor, to the clients bound to you by blood and to foreign clients, to your companions, to travelers and to your slaves. God does not love the presumptuous and boastful man.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 4, 36 [Kazimirski IV, 40] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869. The “clients” are, in Arabic, the neighbors, near or distant.</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

All the traditions place compassion at the summit of the virtues. The Abrahamic religions contemplate it **in God** and ask man to imitate it: “Be merciful, as your Father is merciful”; the Qur'an opens every sura with divine mercy, and the Hebrew Bible proclaims a God “gracious and merciful” whose “pity extends to all His creatures.” The traditions of India cultivate it as a disposition **toward all beings**, and the Metta Sutta describes it through the universal image of the mother watching over her only child.

#### Where They Differ

In Judaism, Christianity and Islam, human compassion answers that of God; the Gospel expressly extends it to the stranger and the enemy. In Hinduism, it flows from the vision of the same Self in all; in Buddhism, it is a state of mind that one develops through meditation, without reference to a God. The Indian traditions explicitly extend it to animals.

</div>

## 5. Non-Violence and Peace

All the traditions condemn murder and exalt peace. They do not all draw the same consequence from this condemnation: some go as far as refusing all violence, others regulate the use of force. The texts that follow state the ideal; the synthesis will also state the limits.

### Hinduism

Non-violence, *ahimsa*, is one of the highest virtues of Hinduism. The Gita places it at the head of the qualities of the man “born to a divine condition”; Burnouf renders the word as “the peaceable temper”:

> The peaceable temper, truthfulness, gentleness, renunciation, inner calm, benevolence, pity for living beings, peace of heart, mildness, modesty, gravity;

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVI, 2 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Abstention from cruelty is the highest Religion. Abstention from cruelty is the highest self-mastery. Abstention from cruelty is the highest gift. Abstention from cruelty is the highest penance. Abstention from cruelty is the highest sacrifice. […] He who abstains from cruelty is the father and mother of all creatures.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata XIII (Anushasana-parva), section 116 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

The Yoga-sutras describe its fruit:

> As soon as he is firmly established in abstention from harming, his presence causes enmity to cease.

<p class="ref">— Yoga-sutra of Patanjali II, 35 · working translation after Woods, 1914</p>

### Judaism

> You shall not commit homicide.

<p class="ref">— Exodus 20, 13 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Mishnah draws from the creation of a single man, Adam, the infinite value of each life:

> This is why God created Adam alone […], to show us that he who saves a single human being saves a whole world, and that he who destroys a man is to be likened to one who destroys a whole world. The fact that God created a single man also had the purpose of showing that all men are brothers, and of preventing anyone from being able to believe himself superior to an individual of another nation […].

<p class="ref">— Mishnah, Sanhedrin 4, 5 · tr. Moïse Schwab, 1878-1890. Schwab follows the universal form of the text; the printed editions of the Hebrew read “a single soul of Israel.”</p>

The prophets announce the end of war:

> […] they then shall forge from their swords plowshares and from their spears pruning hooks; one people shall no longer draw the sword against another people, and the art of war shall no longer be learned.

<p class="ref">— Isaiah 2, 4 · tr. Zadoc Kahn (excerpt)</p>

> Hillel said: Be of the disciples of Aaron, loving peace and pursuing peace, loving creatures and bringing them near to the Torah.

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot (Sayings of the Fathers) 1, 12 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>

### Buddhism

The first of the five precepts that the Buddha gives the layman concerns the life of every being:

> Let him not kill, nor cause to kill any living being, and let him not approve of others killing, having refrained from doing harm to all creatures, both those that are strong and those that tremble in the world.

<p class="ref">— Dhammika Sutta, Sutta Nipata 393 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

> Victory breeds hatred, for the vanquished feels pain. He who lives in peace is happy, no longer thinking of victory or of defeat.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 201 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

> Blessed are those who bring about peace, for they shall be called sons of God!

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5, 9 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> You have heard that it was said: an eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth. But I say to you, do not resist the wicked one. If someone strikes you on the right cheek, offer him the other also. […] You have heard that it was said: You shall love your neighbor, and you shall hate your enemy. But I say to you: Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and who persecute you, […]

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5, 38-39 and 43-44 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

At the moment of his arrest, Jesus refuses to be defended by the sword:

> Then Jesus said to him: Put your sword back in its place; for all those who take the sword shall perish by the sword.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 26, 52 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Do not let yourself be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good.

<p class="ref">— Romans 12, 21 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur'an takes up a law that it says was given to the children of Israel, and which makes every life the equal of all humanity:

> He who has killed a man, without that man having killed a man or sown disorder in the land, shall be regarded as the murderer of the human race; and he who has restored life to a man shall be regarded as if he had restored life to the whole human race.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 5, 32 [Kazimirski V, 35] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> No compulsion in religion.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 2, 256 [Kazimirski II, 257] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Evil and good cannot walk together. Render good for evil, and you will see your enemy change into a protector and a friend.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 41, 34 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> If they incline to peace, you shall incline to it also, and you shall put your trust in God, for He hears and knows all.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 8, 61 [Kazimirski VIII, 63] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

All affirm the **sacredness of life** and condemn murder. The Mishnah teaches that “he who saves a single human being saves a whole world,” and the Qur'an takes up this law, saying it was given to the children of Israel; the Mahabharata makes abstention from cruelty “the highest Religion”; the first Buddhist precept protects every living being. All exalt peace, and several ask us to **render good for evil**: “Render good for evil” (Qur'an), “overcome evil with good” (Paul), “it is not hatred that makes hatred cease” (Dhammapada).

#### Where They Differ

The ideal is shared; the relationship to force is not. Buddhism is the most radical, and Hinduism makes *ahimsa* a major virtue — but the Gita unfolds on a battlefield, and there Krishna reminds the warrior of his duty to fight. Jesus commands the love of enemies, but the Churches developed a doctrine of “just war.” The Hebrew Bible knows the wars of Israel alongside the prophetic hope of a universal peace. The Qur'an, finally, authorizes defensive combat and regulates it (2, 190; 22, 39): its demand for peace is not an absolute pacifism.

</div>

## 6. Forgiveness

To answer offense with offense keeps the cycle of hatred going without end. The five traditions know this, and all invite us to break that cycle. But they do not give forgiveness the same source: a gift of God to be imitated for some, an inner liberation from resentment for others.

### Hinduism

Exiled and humiliated, King Yudhishthira refuses to yield to the anger his wife Draupadi urges upon him, and answers her with a eulogy of forgiveness:

> Forgiveness is virtue; forgiveness is sacrifice; forgiveness is the Vedas, forgiveness is Revelation. He who knows this is capable of forgiving everything. Forgiveness is Brahma; forgiveness is Truth […] and it is by forgiveness that the universe is upheld. […] Forgiveness is the strength of the strong; forgiveness is sacrifice; forgiveness is peace of mind.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata III (Vana-parva), section 29 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

> Compassion is the highest virtue. Forgiveness is the highest strength. Self-knowledge is the highest knowledge. There is nothing higher than truth.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata XII (Shanti-parva), section 330 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

The Laws of Manu count forgiveness among the ten marks of duty; their translator of 1833 renders it with a phrase that recalls the Gospel:

> Resignation, the act of returning good for evil, temperance, probity, purity, the restraint of the senses, knowledge of the Sastras, that of the supreme Soul, truthfulness and abstention from anger: such are the ten virtues in which duty consists.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu VI, 92 · tr. Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

### Judaism

Judaism lays down a demanding rule: God forgives what offends Him, but He does not forgive in the place of the one who has been wronged.

> Man’s faults toward God, the Day of Atonement atones for them; man’s faults toward his neighbor, the Day of Atonement does not atone for them, until he has appeased his neighbor.

<p class="ref">— Mishnah, Yoma 8, 9 · working translation from the Hebrew</p>

> What god is your equal [Lord], You who pardon iniquities, who grant grace to offenses […]? You who do not keep Your anger forever, because You delight in benevolence? Yes, You will take us back in pity, You will stifle our iniquities, You will cast all our sins into the depths of the sea.

<p class="ref">— Micah 7, 18-19 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

Joseph, once sold by his brothers, refuses to take revenge when they are at his mercy:

> Joseph answered them: Be without fear; for am I in the place of God? You, you had plotted evil against me: God contrived it for good […]. Therefore, be without fear: I will take care of you and your families. And he reassured them and spoke to their hearts.

<p class="ref">— Genesis 50, 19-21 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Hatred stirs up quarrels; love covers all faults.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 10, 12 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

The opening verses of the Dhammapada describe the very mechanism of resentment, and the way out of it:

> “I was insulted, I was struck, I was overthrown, I was robbed!” — Those who let themselves go on speaking thus never cease to hate.
>
> “I was insulted, I was struck, I was overthrown, I was robbed!” — Those who do not let themselves go on speaking thus, cease to hate.
>
> “What makes hatreds cease here below is by no means hatred, but rather the absence of hatred.” — That is an axiom as old as the world.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 3-5 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> Let no one deceive another, let him not despise (anyone) in any place, let him not wish ill upon another out of anger or out of resentment.

<p class="ref">— Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipata 147 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

### Christianity

> If you forgive men their offenses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you; but if you do not forgive men, your Father will not forgive your offenses either.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6, 14-15 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Then Peter came to him, and said: Lord, how many times shall I forgive my brother, when he sins against me? Shall it be up to seven times? Jesus said to him: I do not say to you up to seven times, but up to seventy times seven times.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 18, 21-22 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

On the cross, Jesus himself intercedes for those who put him to death:

> Jesus said: Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.

<p class="ref">— Luke 23, 34 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

> […] let them rather forgive them *their faults* and pass over them. Would you not *also* wish that God forgive you? He is indulgent and merciful.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 24, 22 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

The Qur'an recognizes the right to a retaliation equal to the wrong suffered, but it places forgiveness above it:

> And render for evil an equal evil. He however who forgives and is reconciled *with his adversary,* God shall owe him a reward; for He does not love the oppressors.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 42, 40 [Kazimirski XLII, 38] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> It is the wisdom of life to bear with patience and to forgive.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 42, 43 [Kazimirski XLII, 41] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

The five traditions see in vengeance an endless cycle, and in forgiveness the only means of breaking it. All present forgiveness not as a weakness but as **a strength**: “the strength of the strong” (Mahabharata), “the wisdom of life” (Qur'an). Several link the forgiveness one grants to the forgiveness one hopes for: “if you forgive men, your heavenly Father will also forgive you”; “would you not also wish that God forgive you?”

#### Where They Differ

Christianity asks for a forgiveness **without limit** (“seventy times seven times”) and sees its example in Jesus forgiving his executioners. Islam recognizes the right to a reparation equal to the wrong suffered, but places forgiveness above it. Judaism binds forgiveness closely to **repentance**, and lays down a rule with no equivalent so clear elsewhere: God does not forgive in the place of the victim; one must first “appease one’s neighbor.” Buddhism does not speak of a forgiveness granted by God: it describes the letting go of resentment, which frees the offended party first of all — the evil deed itself bears its fruit according to the law of karma.

</div>

## 7. Giving and Care for the Poor

No religion is content with prayer alone. All ask us to share, and almost all name the same beneficiaries: the poor, the orphan, the widow, the stranger, the traveler. Several add a strikingly similar requirement: to give without ostentation, and without humiliating the one who receives.

### Hinduism

> What one gives, let one give it with faith, not without faith; with joy, with modesty, with awe, with benevolence.

<p class="ref">— Taittiriya Upanishad I, 11, 3 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

The Gita distinguishes the disinterested gift from the calculated gift:

> A gift made with the sense of duty, to a man who cannot repay, a gift made in due time and place and according to merit, is a gift of truth. A present made in the hope of a return or a reward, and as if grudgingly, proceeds from desire.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVII, 20-21 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

An Upanishad relates that the creator god uttered, for the gods, for men and for the demons, a single syllable, “Da,” which each understood according to what he lacked; men heard in it *datta*, “give”:

> The divine voice of the thunder repeats the same thing: Da Da Da, that is to say: Subdue yourselves, Give, Be merciful. Let this triad therefore be taught: self-mastery, giving and mercy.

<p class="ref">— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad V, 2, 3 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

### Judaism

> If there be among you a needy man, from among your brothers, in one of your towns […], you shall not harden your heart, nor shut your hand to your needy brother. Open your hand to him rather! […] Now, there will always be needy people in the land; that is why I give you this charge: open, open your hand to your brother, to the poor, to the needy who shall be in your land!

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 15, 7-8 and 11 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

God defines Himself by the care of the most vulnerable:

> who does justice to the orphan and the widow; who shows His love to the stranger, by assuring him bread and clothing. You shall love the stranger, you who were strangers in the land of Egypt!

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 10, 18-19 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> To give to the poor is to lend to God, who pays each his due.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 19, 17 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud places personal acts of kindness even higher than almsgiving:

> In three respects benevolence is superior to almsgiving: — almsgiving is only the payment of money, but benevolence can also be exercised through personal service. Almsgiving can be given only to the poor, but benevolence can be shown no less toward the rich. Almsgiving is limited to the living, but benevolence can extend to the dead as well as to the living.

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 49b · after Hershon, 1880, library translation</p>

### Buddhism

> Speak the truth; do not give way to anger; give, to whoever implores you, from the little you have. On these three conditions, you will draw near to the gods.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 224 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> If creatures knew, O monks, the fruit of the distribution of gifts as I know it, they would not eat without having given, and the stain of selfishness would not mark their hearts deeply. Whatever the smallest mouthful, the smallest morsel they possess, if there were someone to receive it, they would not eat without first having shared it.

<p class="ref">— Itivuttaka 26 · working translation after Moore, 1908</p>
### Christianity

At the Last Judgment, according to the Gospel of Matthew, Christ identifies himself with the one who was helped:

> For I was hungry, and you gave me food; I was thirsty, and you gave me drink; I was a stranger, and you took me in; I was naked, and you clothed me; I was sick, and you visited me; I was in prison, and you came to me. […] And the king will answer them: Truly I say to you, whenever you did these things to one of the least of these my brothers, you did them to me.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 25, 35-36 and 40 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Pure and undefiled religion, before God our Father, consists in visiting orphans and widows in their affliction, and in keeping oneself unstained by the world.

<p class="ref">— James 1, 27 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> But when you give alms, let not your left hand know what your right hand is doing, so that your alms may be given in secret; and your Father, who sees in secret, will reward you.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6, 3-4 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The “verse of piety” refuses to reduce religion to ritual:

> Piety does not consist in turning your faces toward the east or the west. Pious is he who believes in God and in the last day, in the angels and in the Book, in the prophets; who, for the love of God, gives of his substance to his kin, to orphans, to the poor, to travelers and to those who ask; who ransoms captives, who observes prayer, who gives alms, fulfills the engagements he contracts, who is patient in adversity […].

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 2, 177 [Kazimirski II, 172] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> An honest word, the forgiveness of offenses, are worth more than alms followed by pain caused to the one who receives them. God is rich and clement.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 2, 263 [Kazimirski II, 265] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

A short sura even makes contempt for the orphan and the poor the mark of the one who denies religion:

> What do you think of him who calls this religion a lie?
>
> It is he who repels the orphan,
>
> Who does not urge others to feed the poor.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 107, 1-3 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

The five traditions make giving a religious duty, and name almost the same beneficiaries: **the poor, the orphan, the widow, the stranger, the traveler**. More striking still, they agree on the **manner** of giving: in secret, without “your left hand knowing what your right hand is doing” (Gospel); without following alms with reproaches (Qur'an); with joy and modesty (Upanishad); without expecting anything in return (Gita); sharing even the last mouthful (Itivuttaka).

#### Where They Differ

The status of the gift differs. In Islam, the legal alms (*zakat*) is an obligatory and quantified pillar, distinct from voluntary alms. In Judaism, the very word for charity, *tzedakah*, means “justice”: to give to the poor is to return to them what is theirs. Christianity sees Christ himself in the poor person who is helped. Buddhism makes giving an exercise in detachment that tears out “the stain of selfishness”; Hinduism judges its worth by the intention, the moment and the person.

</div>

## 8. Honoring Father and Mother

The respect owed to parents is doubtless the duty most unanimously taught by the religions of the world. In the Bible as in the Qur'an, it follows immediately after the duties toward God; in the texts of India, parents are treated as divinities or as the worthiest of masters.

### Hinduism

On the day the student leaves his master after studying the Veda, the master gives him his final counsel:

> Do not neglect the works owed to the Gods and to the Fathers! Let your mother be to you as a god! Let your father be to you as a god! Let your teacher be to you as a god! Let your guest be to you as a god!

<p class="ref">— Taittiriya Upanishad I, 11, 2 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

> A teacher is more venerable than ten assistant tutors; a father, than a hundred teachers; a mother is more venerable than a thousand fathers.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu II, 145 · tr. Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

> By his respect for his mother he obtains this lower world; by his respect for his father, the intermediate world, that of the atmosphere; by his submission to the orders of his spiritual guide, he reaches the heavenly world of Brahma.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu II, 233 · tr. Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

### Judaism

> Honor your father and your mother, so that your days may be prolonged on the land that the LORD your God will grant you.

<p class="ref">— Exodus 20, 12 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Revere, each of you, your mother and your father, and keep my sabbaths: I am the LORD your God. […] Rise before a white head, and honor the person of the old man: fear your God! I am the LORD.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19, 3 and 32 · tr. Zadoc Kahn. Here the mother is named before the father.</p>

The Talmud explains why this commandment touches God himself:

> The rabbis teach that there are three partners in the making of a man: God, the father and the mother.

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Niddah 31a · after Hershon, 1880, tr. by the library (excerpt)</p>

And the model of filial piety it proposes is, remarkably, a non-Jew:

> Rav Ulla was once asked: “How far is one bound to honor one's father and mother?” To which he replied: “See what a Gentile of Ashkelon once did, Dammah ben Nethinah by name. The sages once needed goods worth sixty myriads, which they were ready to pay for at the price asked, but the key to the storehouse happened to be under the pillow of his father, who was fast asleep, and Dammah would not disturb him.”

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Kiddushin 31a · after Hershon, 1880, tr. by the library</p>

### Buddhism

> Monks, there are two persons, I declare, who can never be repaid. Which two? Mother and father. Even if one were to carry one's mother on one shoulder and one's father on the other, and were to live thus a hundred years […], one could not repay one's parents. […] Why is that? Monks, parents do much for their children: they raise them, they feed them, they bring them into this world.

<p class="ref">— Anguttara Nikaya II, 4, 2 · working translation after Woodward, 1932</p>

In the teaching he gives to the young layman Sigala, the Buddha describes reciprocal duties:

> In five ways a child should serve his parents […]: — Supported by them once, I shall now be their support; I shall perform the duties that fall to them; I shall maintain the lineage and tradition of my family; I shall make myself worthy of my inheritance. […] In five ways the parents thus served […] show their love for him: they turn him from vice, exhort him to virtue, train him for a profession, arrange a suitable marriage for him and, when the time comes, hand over his inheritance to him.

<p class="ref">— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya III, 189-190 · working translation after T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids, 1921</p>

> To serve one's mother and father, to protect child and wife, and a peaceful occupation, this is the highest blessing.

<p class="ref">— Mahamangala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 261 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

### Christianity

> Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. Honor your father and your mother (this is the first commandment with a promise), so that you may be happy and live long on the earth. And you, fathers, do not provoke your children, but bring them up with correction and instruction in the Lord.

<p class="ref">— Ephesians 6, 1-4 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

Jesus reproaches some of his contemporaries for having excused themselves, under the pretext of a religious offering, from helping their parents:

> He answered them: And you, why do you transgress the commandment of God for the sake of your tradition? For God said: Honor your father and your mother; and: Whoever curses his father or his mother shall be put to death.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 15, 3-4 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

> God has ordained that you worship none but him, and that you behave well toward your father and mother, whether one of them has reached old age or both have attained it, and they remain with you. Beware of showing them contempt, of reproaching them. Speak to them with respect.
>
> Be humble toward them and full of tenderness, and address this prayer to God: Lord, have mercy on them, just as *they had mercy on me*, as they raised me when I was very small.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 17, 23-24 [Kazimirski XVII, 24-25] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

A famous hadith gives the mother a place apart:

> “A man came to the Messenger of God and said: ‘O Messenger of God, who has the greatest right to my good companionship?’ He replied: ‘Your mother.’ — ‘And then?’ — ‘Your mother.’ — ‘And then?’ — ‘Your mother.’ — ‘And then?’ — ‘Then your father.’”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 5971) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

This is the most unanimously taught duty. In the Bible as in the Qur'an, honoring one's parents comes **immediately after the duties toward God**; the Upanishad asks that one hold one's mother and father “as a god.” Several traditions give **the mother** a place apart (“Your mother, your mother, your mother,” says the hadith; “a mother is more venerable than a thousand fathers,” say the Laws of Manu), and several say that one can never repay one's parents for what one has received from them.

#### Where They Differ

Some traditions insist on **reciprocity**: Paul asks fathers not to provoke their children, the Buddha enumerates the duties of parents toward their child. The Qur'an sets a limit: one does not obey parents who push one toward idolatry, while continuing to treat them with kindness. Hinduism extends this respect to the spiritual master and to the guest, and prolongs it after death through the worship of ancestors.

</div>

# Part Four — Conduct

<p class="part-intro">After one's neighbor, oneself. Speaking the truth, not taking what belongs to another, governing one's desires and one's anger, guarding against pride: the everyday virtues are also those on which the Scriptures agree most simply.</p>

## 9. Truth and Right Speech

Lying, slander and malicious gossip are condemned everywhere. But several traditions go further and make speech a spiritual domain in its own right: the tongue can bless or destroy, and whoever masters it masters himself entirely.

### Hinduism

The motto of the Republic of India is taken from an Upanishad:

> Truth triumphs, not falsehood; by truth the path is laid out, the way of the gods, by which the sages of old, their desires fulfilled, advance to where the supreme abode of Truth is found.

<p class="ref">— Mundaka Upanishad III, 1, 6 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

> Speak the truth! Do your duty! Do not neglect the study of the Veda! […] Do not swerve from the truth! Do not swerve from duty!

<p class="ref">— Taittiriya Upanishad I, 11, 1 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

> Measured, truthful speech, full of gentleness, and the practice of pious readings are the austerity of speech.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVII, 15 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Let him speak the truth, let him say things that give pleasure, let him not declare a disagreeable truth, and let him not utter an obliging lie; such is the eternal law.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu IV, 138 · tr. Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

### Judaism

> The LORD abhors lying lips; but he loves those who act with loyalty.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 12, 22 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Do not go about spreading evil among your people, do not be indifferent to the danger of your neighbor: I am the LORD.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19, 16 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Keep your tongue from evil, and your lips from treacherous speech; turn away from evil and do good, seek peace and pursue it.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 34, 14-15 [Segond 34, 13-14] · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud calls slander “the third tongue,” because it claims three victims:

> The third tongue (that is, slander) wounds three parties: the slanderer himself, the one who receives the slander, and the person slandered.

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Arakhin 15b · after Hershon, 1880, tr. by the library</p>

### Buddhism

“Right speech” is the third limb of the Noble Eightfold Path, and the Buddha defines it plainly:

> And what, bhikkhus, is right speech? Abstaining from lying, from slander, from harsh words and from idle chatter. This is what is called right speech.

<p class="ref">— Mahasatipatthana Sutta, Digha Nikaya II, 312 · working translation after T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids, 1910</p>

> Better a single word that has meaning than a thousand words devoid of meaning, if it brings calm to the one who hears it.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 100 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

> Let your word be yes, yes, no, no; whatever is added to this comes from the evil one.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5, 37 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Therefore, renounce falsehood, and let each of you speak the truth to his neighbor; for we are members of one another.

<p class="ref">— Ephesians 4, 25 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Likewise, the tongue is a small member, and it boasts of great things. Behold, how a small fire can set a great forest ablaze! The tongue also is a fire; it is the world of iniquity. […] With it we bless the Lord our Father, and with it we curse men made in the image of God. From the same mouth come blessing and cursing. My brothers, this ought not to be so.

<p class="ref">— James 3, 5-6 and 9-10 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

> O believers! fear the Lord; speak with uprightness.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 33, 70 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Do not clothe the truth in the robe of falsehood; Do not hide the truth when you know it.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 2, 42 [Kazimirski II, 39] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

The Qur'an compares malicious gossip to an act that all judge monstrous:

> O you who believe! avoid too frequent suspicion; there are suspicions that are sins; do not seek to spy on the steps of others, do not speak ill of one another; which of you would wish to eat the flesh of his dead brother? — Does it disgust you? — Then fear God.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 49, 12 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> “Truthfulness leads to piety, and piety leads to Paradise; a man keeps speaking the truth until he becomes a truthful one. Lying leads to perversity, and perversity leads to the Fire; a man keeps lying until he is recorded with God as a liar.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 6094) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

All the traditions condemn lying, false witness and malicious gossip, and all recognize in speech a **formidable power**: the tongue “is a fire” (James), gossip amounts to “eating the flesh of one's dead brother” (Qur'an), “right speech” is a limb of the Buddhist Noble Path. All also make truth a **path**: “truth triumphs” (Upanishad), “truthfulness leads to piety” (hadith).

#### Where They Differ

The texts do not set the limit in the same place. The Laws of Manu ask that one not “declare a disagreeable truth”: truth is there subordinated to kindness. Jesus asks that one renounce oaths, because a simple word should suffice. Judaism forbids speaking ill of another **even when it is true**, and the Talmud counts three victims of slander. Buddhism also counts **idle chatter** among the faults of speech.

</div>

## 10. Honesty and Justice

“You shall not steal”: the prohibition appears in the Decalogue, among the five precepts of Buddhism, in the lists of duties of Hinduism. But the texts do not stop at armed robbery. They aim at the merchant's falsified scales, the shortened measure, the deposit not returned — ordinary dishonesty, the kind anyone can commit without thinking himself a thief.

### Hinduism

Beyond the duties proper to each class of society, the Laws of Manu set out a duty common to all:

> To refrain from doing harm, to always speak the truth, to abstain from all theft, to be pure, and to restrain one's organs, this is in sum the duty prescribed by Manu to the four classes.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu X, 63 · tr. Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali make these same prohibitions the first step of the spiritual life:

> Abstention from harming, abstention from lying, from theft, from incontinence and from the acceptance of gifts: such are the abstentions.

<p class="ref">— Yoga Sutras of Patanjali II, 30 · working translation after Woods, 1914</p>

### Judaism

> You shall not commit theft, nor denial nor fraud to the detriment of your neighbor.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19, 11 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Do not commit iniquity in matters of judgment, of weights and of measures. Have exact scales, exact weights, an exact ephah, an exact hin: I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19, 35-36 · tr. Zadoc Kahn. The ephah and the hin (*hîn*) are measures of grain and of liquids.</p>

According to the Talmud, the first question put to a man before the heavenly court concerns neither his prayers nor his study:

> At the hour when a man is brought to judgment, he is asked: Did you conduct your business with integrity? Did you set fixed times for the Torah? Did you occupy yourself with founding a family? Did you hope for deliverance? […]

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a (saying of Rava) · working translation from the Aramaic</p>

### Buddhism

> Let the disciple abstain from taking, in any place whatsoever, anything whatsoever that has not been given to him, knowing (that it belongs to another); let him not cause anyone to take, let him not approve those who take; let him avoid every (kind of) theft.

<p class="ref">— Dhammika Sutta, Sutta Nipata 394 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

The Dhammapada gathers in two stanzas the breaches of the five precepts, and states their consequence:

> He who destroys a life, who speaks lying words, who takes in this world what is not given to him, who approaches another's wife,
>
> And who gives himself over to strong drink, — that man, in this world, tears up his own roots himself.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 246-247 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

> Let him who stole steal no more; but rather let him labor, doing with his hands what is good, so that he may have something to give to him who is in need.

<p class="ref">— Ephesians 4, 28 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

John the Baptist, who prepares the coming of Jesus, gives each person a rule of justice fitted to his trade:

> Tax collectors also came to be baptized, and they said to him: Master, what must we do? He answered them: Exact nothing beyond what has been ordered you. Soldiers also asked him: And we, what must we do? He answered them: Commit neither extortion nor fraud against anyone, and be content with your pay.

<p class="ref">— Luke 3, 12-14 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Indeed, the commandments: You shall not commit adultery, you shall not kill, you shall not steal, you shall not covet, and whatever others there may be, are summed up in this word: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. Love does no harm to the neighbor: love is therefore the fulfillment of the law.

<p class="ref">— Romans 13, 9-10 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

> Woe to those who falsify the measure or the weight,
>
> Who, when buying, demand a full measure,
>
> And who, when they measure or weigh for others, cheat them!

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 83, 1-3 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> When you measure, fill the measure. Weigh with a just balance. This is better, and fairer in the final result.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 17, 35 [Kazimirski XVII, 37] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> God commands you to return the deposit to whom it belongs, and to judge your fellows with equity. It is a fine deed that God recommends to you. He hears and sees all.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 4, 58 [Kazimirski IV, 61] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

The prohibition of theft appears in all the fundamental lists: the Decalogue, the five Buddhist precepts, the abstentions of yoga, the “duty common to the four classes” of the Laws of Manu. All the traditions also aim at **ordinary fraud** — the falsified measure, the rigged scales, the deposit not returned — and the Qur'an, like the Torah, makes just measure a religious matter.

#### Where They Differ

The emphases differ: Buddhism forbids not only taking, but causing others to take and approving theft; yoga goes as far as abstaining from **accepting gifts**; Paul turns the prohibition into a positive duty — working “so as to have something to give” — and brings it wholly back to love of neighbor.

</div>

## 11. Self-Mastery: Fasting, Desires, Anger

All the traditions see in the man given over to his appetites and his angers a being who is not free. All propose a discipline for taking back the reins: fasting, temperance, vigilance. And several say, almost in the same terms, that victory over oneself is the greatest of all.

### Hinduism

The Gita describes, with astonishing precision, the chain that leads from a simple glance to inner ruin:

> In the man who contemplates the objects of the senses, an inclination toward them is born; from this inclination is born desire; from desire, violent appetite; From this appetite, disturbance of thought; from this disturbance, the wandering of memory; from the ruin of memory, the loss of reason; and by this loss, he is lost.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 62-63 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Hell has three gates through which they are lost: lust, anger and greed. They must therefore be avoided.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVI, 21 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

But it rejects the excesses of asceticism as much as those of appetite:

> The divine Union is neither for him who eats too much, nor for him who eats nothing; it is neither for him who sleeps long, nor for him who is always awake, Arjuna. The holy Union, which takes away all ills, is for him who eats in measure, recreates himself in measure, acts, sleeps and wakes in measure.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita VI, 16-17 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

The only fast prescribed by the Torah is that of the Day of Atonement. But the prophet read that day in the synagogue recalls what God expects of a fast:

> Is this a fast that can be pleasing to me, a day when a man mortifies himself? […] But this is the fast that I love: to break the chains of injustice, to untie the bonds of every yoke, to send free those who are oppressed, to shatter at last every servitude; then further, to share your bread with the hungry, to take into your house the wretched without shelter; when you see a naked man, to cover him […]!

<p class="ref">— Isaiah 58, 5-7 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Rend your hearts and not your garments, and return to the LORD, your God, for he is clement, merciful, slow to anger and abounding in grace.

<p class="ref">— Joel 2, 13 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> He who resists anger prevails over the hero; he who governs his passions, over a taker of cities.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 16, 32 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

> One might, in a single encounter, vanquish thousands upon thousands of men: to vanquish oneself alone is the most glorious of victories.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 103 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> Let one rid oneself of anger! Let one rid oneself of pride! Let one shake off all one's bonds! […]
>
> He whose anger has been given free rein, but who holds it back like a chariot in motion, — that man I call a charioteer. The rest of men merely hold the reins.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 221-222 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

> When you fast, do not put on a sad look, like the hypocrites, who disfigure their faces to show men that they are fasting. […] But when you fast, anoint your head and wash your face, so as not to show men that you are fasting, but your Father who is there in the secret place; and your Father, who sees in secret, will reward you.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6, 16-18 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control; the law is not against these things.

<p class="ref">— Galatians 5, 22-23 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> So let every man be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not accomplish the justice of God.

<p class="ref">— James 1, 19-20 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur'an institutes the fast of the month of Ramadan while expressly linking it to the earlier religions:

> O believers! fasting is prescribed for you, just as it was prescribed for those who came before you. Fear the Lord.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 2, 183 [Kazimirski II, 179] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> To those who give alms in ease as in hardship, who know how to master their anger, and who forgive the men *who offend them*. Truly God loves those who act with kindness.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 3, 134 [Kazimirski III, 128] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> “The strong man is not he who throws others down in wrestling; the strong man is only he who masters himself when he is angry.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 6114) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

All the traditions see in the man ruled by his appetites and his anger a being who is not free, and several say in the same terms that **victory over oneself is the greatest**: “to vanquish oneself alone is the most glorious of victories” (Dhammapada), “the strong man is he who masters himself when he is angry” (hadith), “he who resists anger prevails over the hero” (Proverbs). All know **fasting**, and the Qur'an expressly says it was “prescribed for those who came before you.” Hinduism and Buddhism recommend the same **middle way**, between indulgence and mortification.

#### Where They Differ

Fasting is a fixed obligation in Islam (the month of Ramadan) and in Judaism (the day of Yom Kippur in particular); it is a practice of piety, discreet and freer, in the Gospel; the Gita blames the austerity that tortures the body. For Paul, self-mastery is a “fruit of the Spirit,” a gift received more than a conquest. Buddhism and Islam furthermore forbid intoxicating drinks.

</div>
## 12. Humility

Pride is, in each of the five traditions, a major obstacle: it closes man off from God, from others, and from the truth about himself. Humility there is not a weakness but a clear-sightedness — that of the man who sees the speck in his own eye before looking for it in his neighbor’s.

### Hinduism

Among the qualities that make up true knowledge, the Gita names first the absence of pride, which Burnouf renders as “modesty”:

> Modesty, sincerity, gentleness, patience, uprightness, respect for the teacher, purity, constancy, mastery over oneself;

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XIII, 7 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Hypocrisy, pride, vanity, anger, harshness of speech, ignorance — such, son of Pritha, are the marks of him who is born into the condition of the Asuras.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVI, 4 · tr. Burnouf, 1861. The Asuras are the demonic powers.</p>

> Let a man, having cast them all out, walk without desires, without greed, without pride; he walks toward peace.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 71 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

A prophet sums up the whole of religion in a single sentence, and humility is part of it:

> Man, you have been told what is good, what the Lord asks of you: nothing but to practice justice, to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God!

<p class="ref">— Micah 6, 8 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Pride goes before ruin; arrogance is the forerunner of the fall.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 16, 18 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud explains why, in the controversies, the law followed the school of Hillel rather than that of Shammai:

> His disciples were gentle and patient, for while upholding their own rulings, they also set forth those maintained by the school of Shammai, and often even mentioned the positions of the school of Shammai first […]. This teaches us that whoever humbles himself, God will raise up; and whoever raises himself up, God will bring low. Whoever pursues greatness, greatness will flee from him; and whoever flees from greatness, greatness will pursue him.

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 13b · after Hershon, 1880, library translation (excerpt)</p>

### Buddhism

> The fool who knows that he is a fool is, in that at least, a wise man. But the fool who believes himself wise — of him it is said: “he is a fool.”

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 63 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> It is not on the transgressions of others, on the deeds or omissions of others, that he should fix his attention, but on what he himself has done or left undone.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 50 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> Easy to see is the fault of another, hard to see one’s own. The faults of others one brings out as much as one can; one’s own, by contrast, one hides as the cheat hides the kali from his partner.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 252 · tr. Hû, 1878. The “kali” is the losing throw in the game of dice.</p>

### Christianity

> Blessed are the poor in spirit, for the kingdom of heaven is theirs!

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5, 3 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> For whoever exalts himself will be brought low, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.

<p class="ref">— Luke 14, 11 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Do nothing out of a spirit of faction or out of vainglory, but let humility make you regard others as being above yourselves.

<p class="ref">— Philippians 2, 3 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

> The servants of the Merciful are those who walk with modesty upon this earth, and who say: PEACE BE WITH YOU, to the ignorant who address them.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 25, 63 [Kazimirski XXV, 64] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

The sage Luqman, a figure of proverbial wisdom, counsels his son thus:

> Do not twist your mouth *in disdain* for men; let your walk not be proud, for God does not love the presumptuous, boastful man.
>
> Seek to moderate your step and to lower your voice, for the most unpleasant of voices is indeed the voice of the donkey.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 31, 18-19 [Kazimirski XXXI, 17-18] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Do not walk pompously upon the earth; you could neither split it in two nor equal the height of the mountains.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 17, 37 [Kazimirski XVII, 39] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Pride is everywhere the enemy. All the traditions ask us to **walk with modesty** upon the earth, to see our own faults before those of others — the Dhammapada here meets the Gospel image of the speck and the beam — and promise exaltation to the one who humbles himself.

#### Where They Differ

Christian humility has a unique model: the self-abasement of God himself in Jesus, who “emptied himself.” In Hinduism, pride is *ahankara*, the sense of being a separate “I,” which the sage abandons. In Buddhism, it is one of the fetters that bind one to the cycle of rebirths, and its root is the illusion of a permanent self.

</div>

# Part Five — The Inner Life

<p class="part-intro">Deeds are not enough. All the traditions also ask us to turn inward: to pray or to meditate, to purify the intention from which deeds are born, and to loosen the grip of goods that pass away.</p>

## 13. Prayer and Meditation

One prays to God in the religions born of Abraham, one meditates in Buddhism, one does both in Hinduism. The gestures and the words differ, and the difference is real: prayer is addressed to someone; Buddhist meditation is addressed to no one. But all these exercises have a striking point in common: they aim to gather a scattered mind, and several texts ask that they be done in secret, far from the eyes of others.

### Hinduism

The most sacred of the Vedic formulas, the Gayatri, has been recited every day by millions of Hindus for three thousand years:

> We adore the noble light of the divine Savitri, who himself calls forth our prayers.

<p class="ref">— Rig-Veda III, 62, 10 · tr. Langlois, 1848-1851</p>

> Lead me from the unreal to the real! Lead me from darkness to light! Lead me from death to immortality!

<p class="ref">— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad I, 3, 28 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

The Gita also teaches silent meditation, apart, with the mind gathered:

> Let the Yogi always practice his devotion alone, apart, without company, master of his thought, stripped of hopes. […] The Yogi is like a lamp which, sheltered from the wind, does not flicker when, having subdued his thought, he gives himself to the mystical Union.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita VI, 10 and 19 · tr. Burnouf, 1861. “Union” is Burnouf’s constant rendering of the word *yoga*.</p>

### Judaism

The Shema asks that the word of God accompany every moment of the day:

> These duties that I lay upon you today shall be engraved on your heart. You shall instill them in your children and speak of them, whether in your house or on the road, when you lie down and when you rise.

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 6, 6-7 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> The LORD is near to all who call upon him, to all who call upon him in sincerity.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 145, 18 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> I keep my eyes constantly fixed on the Lord; if he is at my right hand, I shall not falter.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 16, 8 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

The sages warn against prayer that has become routine:

> Rabbi Simeon said: Be attentive in the reading of the Shema and in prayer. And when you pray, do not make your prayer a fixed thing, but a supplication and an entreaty before God, blessed be He […].

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot 2, 13 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>

### Buddhism

> Vigilance is the path that leads to deliverance from death; negligence is the one that leads to death. The vigilant do not die; the negligent are already as if dead.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 21 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> On his wavering, restless thought, hard to contain, hard to master, the intelligent man imposes the same straightness as a fletcher on an arrow.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 33 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

The discourse on mindfulness presents meditation as the way of purification:

> The one way, bhikkhus, that leads to the purification of beings, to the overcoming of sorrow and lamentation, to the extinction of pain and distress, to the acquisition of the right method, to the realization of Nirvana, is that of the four foundations of mindfulness.

<p class="ref">— Mahasatipaṭṭhana Sutta, Digha Nikaya II, 290 · working translation after T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids, 1910</p>

### Christianity

> When you pray, do not be like the hypocrites, who love to pray standing in the synagogues and at the street corners, to be seen by men. Truly I say to you, they receive their reward. But when you pray, go into your room, shut your door, and pray to your Father who is there in the secret place; and your Father, who sees in secret, will repay you.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6, 5-6 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

The prayer taught by Jesus, common to all the Churches:

> Our Father who art in heaven! Hallowed be thy name; thy kingdom come; thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread; forgive us our offenses, as we also forgive those who have offended us; lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6, 9-13 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Be always joyful. Pray without ceasing. Give thanks in all things, for this is the will of God toward you in Jesus Christ.

<p class="ref">— 1 Thessalonians 5, 16-18 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The *Fatiha*, “the Opening,” is recited in every ritual prayer, that is, at least seventeen times a day:

> Praise be to God, master of the universe,
>
> The clement, the merciful,
>
> Sovereign on the day of retribution.
>
> It is you we adore, it is you whose help we implore.
>
> Guide us on the straight path,
>
> On the path of those whom you have showered with your blessings,
>
> Not of those who have incurred your wrath, nor of those who go astray.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 1, 2-7 [Kazimirski I, 1-7] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> When my servants speak to you of me, I shall be near them; I shall answer the prayer of the suppliant who implores me […].

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2, 186 [Kazimirski II, 182] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

> […] What! is it not in the remembrance of God that the hearts of men find rest!

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 13, 28 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt). The “remembrance” is the *dhikr*, the recollection of God.</p>

> […] perform the prayer, for prayer preserves from turpitudes, from blameworthy deeds […].

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 29, 45 [Kazimirski XXIX, 44] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

All the traditions ask us to **gather the mind** and to turn toward what surpasses it, often at regular hours: the *Fatiha* is recited at every Muslim prayer, the Gayatri every day for three thousand years, Jewish prayer marks the rhythm of morning and evening. Several texts recommend **secrecy** and solitude — “go into your room, shut your door” (Gospel), “alone, apart” (Gita) — and all expect prayer to **transform conduct**: “prayer preserves from turpitudes” (Qur’an).

#### Where They Differ

The difference is real: prayer is addressed to **someone**; Buddhist meditation is addressed to no one. Hinduism knows both, the hymn to the god and silent meditation on the Self. The forms vary too: five ritual prayers at fixed hours in Islam, three daily services in Judaism, “pray without ceasing” in Paul, the foundation of mindfulness in Buddhism.

</div>

## 14. The Heart and the Intention

Here is perhaps the deepest convergence. The five traditions refuse to judge a man by the appearance of his deeds and his rites: what counts, they say, is the heart, the mind, the intention from which they proceed. The religious gesture performed without inner purity is worth nothing; the humblest gesture offered from an upright heart is worth much.

### Hinduism

For the God of the Gita, the value of an offering lies not in its price but in the devotion that accompanies it:

> When a leaf, a flower, a fruit, or water is offered to me in adoration, I receive them as food, as a pious offering. So then, what you do, what you eat, what you sacrifice, what you give, what you inflict upon yourself, O son of Kunti, make of it an offering to me.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita IX, 26-27 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

> But every sacrifice, every gift, every penance, every deed performed without Faith is called evil, son of Pritha, and is nothing, neither in this life nor in the other.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVII, 28 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

> He who, having cast out desire, performs works with God in view, is no more stained by sin than the lotus leaf is by water.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita V, 10 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

When Samuel seeks among the sons of Jesse the one whom God has chosen as king, he first lets himself be impressed by appearance:

> But God said to Samuel: “Do not consider his looks or his tall stature; that one I reject. What man sees does not count: man sees only the outside; God looks at the heart.”

<p class="ref">— 1 Samuel 16, 7 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> O God, create in me a pure heart, and cause an upright spirit to be born anew within my breast. […] the sacrifices [pleasing] to God are a contrite spirit; a broken and downcast heart, O God, you do not disdain.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 51, 12 and 19 [Segond 51, 10 and 17] · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud draws from it a brief maxim:

> The Holy One, blessed be He, wants the heart, as it is written: “And the LORD looks at the heart.”

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 106b · working translation from the Aramaic</p>

> Who shall ascend the mountain of the Lord? Who shall stand in his holy dwelling? He whose hands are without stain, whose heart is pure […].

<p class="ref">— Psalm 24, 3-4 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

The Dhammapada opens with this statement, which the tradition holds to be the foundation of the whole teaching:

> In the proper nature of beings, the inner sense holds the first place; the inner sense is what is most eminent; the inner sense makes them what they are. Whoever speaks or acts with a corrupted inner sense — him pain follows, as the wheel follows the foot of the animal that draws (the cart).

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 1 · tr. Hû, 1878. The “inner sense” is the mind (*manas*).</p>

> To abstain from all evil, to do good, to purify one’s thought — such are the commandments of the Buddhas.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 183 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> What use are these braided locks? What use a skirt of goatskin? Within you is utter chaos; you tend only the outside.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 394 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

> Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God!

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5, 8 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> But what comes out of the mouth proceeds from the heart, and that is what defiles a man. For out of the heart come evil thoughts, murders, adulteries, fornications, thefts, false witness, slanders. These are the things that defile a man; but to eat without having washed one’s hands does not defile a man.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 15, 18-20 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> The good man brings good things out of his good treasure, and the evil man brings evil things out of his evil treasure.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 12, 35 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The first hadith of al-Bukhari’s collection, and of an-Nawawi’s *Forty Hadiths*, lays down the principle:

> “Deeds are worth only by their intentions, and each man has only what he intended.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 1) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

> “God looks neither at your appearances nor at your possessions, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by Muslim (no. 2564) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

Concerning the sacrifice of the pilgrimage, the Qur’an says what God truly receives:

> The flesh and the blood of the victims do not reach God; but your piety rises up to him […].

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 22, 37 [Kazimirski XXII, 38] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

> On the day when riches and children will be of no use,
>
> Except to him who comes to God with a sound heart.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 26, 88-89 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

This is perhaps the deepest convergence. The five traditions judge the deed **by the intention that carries it**: “deeds are worth only by their intentions” (hadith), “the inner sense makes beings what they are” (Dhammapada), “out of the heart come evil thoughts” (Gospel), and the God of the Gita receives a simple leaf offered with devotion. All denounce **outward religiosity**: the flesh of the victims “does not reach God” (Qur’an); “you tend only the outside” (Dhammapada); the Hebrew Bible recalls that God looks at the heart.

#### Where They Differ

Judaism and Islam maintain a precise ritual law, while demanding the intention that animates it (*kavana*, *niyya*). Christianity has relativized the rules of ritual purity in favor of purity of heart alone. Hinduism also knows a ritual purity distinct from inner purity. Buddhism makes intention the very heart of the moral act: it is intention that produces karma.

</div>

## 15. Detachment and Impermanence

Everything passes: wealth, health, life itself. The five traditions recall this with an insistence that has nothing morbid about it. It is not a matter of despising the world, but of not being chained to it — of placing one’s “treasure” where it can be neither stolen nor destroyed.

### Hinduism

The heart of the Gita’s teaching is action without attachment to its fruits:

> Be attentive to the performance of works, never to their fruits; do not do the work for the fruit it yields, but do not seek to avoid the work. Steadfast in the mystical Union, perform the work and cast out desire; be equal in success and in reverses; Union is equanimity of soul.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 47-48 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

> The meetings of the elements that cause cold and heat, pleasure and pain, come and go and are not eternal. Bear them, son of Kunti.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 14 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

> All this, all that moves upon the earth, must be enveloped in the Lord (the Self). When you have renounced all that, then you may enjoy. Do not covet anyone’s goods!

<p class="ref">— Isha Upanishad 1 · working translation after Müller, 1879</p>

### Judaism

> The span of our life is seventy years, and, at the most, eighty years; and all their splendor is but toil and misery. For quickly the thread is cut, and we fly away. […] Teach us, then, to count our days, that we may acquire a heart open to wisdom.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 90, 10 and 12 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

Job, stripped in a single day of all his goods, answers with these words, which the Jewish funeral liturgy takes up:

> Naked I came out of my mother’s womb, and naked I shall return there. The LORD had given, the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD!

<p class="ref">— Job 1, 21 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> He who loves money is never satisfied with money; he who loves opulence gets no profit from it: this too is vanity!

<p class="ref">— Ecclesiastes 5, 9 [Segond 5, 10] · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Ben Zoma said: Who is wise? He who learns from every man […]. Who is strong? He who masters his inclination […]. Who is rich? He who rejoices in his portion […].

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot 4, 1 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>

### Buddhism

Impermanence is the first of the three marks of existence, and truly to understand it is already a deliverance:

> “All aggregations are transient.” When one is thoroughly penetrated by this fact, one is delivered from pain. This is the way of purification.
>
> “All aggregations are subject to pain.” When one is thoroughly penetrated by this fact, one is delivered from pain. This is the way of purification.
>
> “All forms are without substantial reality.” When one is thoroughly penetrated by this fact, one is delivered from pain. This is the way of purification.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 277-279 · tr. Hû, 1878. The “aggregations” are compounded things.</p>

These were also the last words of the Buddha:

> Then the Blessed One addressed the brethren and said: “Behold, brethren, I exhort you: decay is inherent in all compounded things! Work out your salvation with diligence!” This was the last word of the Tathagata.

<p class="ref">— Mahaparinibbana Sutta VI, 10 · working translation after Rhys Davids, 1881</p>

### Christianity

> Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy, and where thieves break in and steal; but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where moth and rust do not destroy, and where thieves neither break in nor steal. For where your treasure is, there also will your heart be.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6, 19-21 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Guard yourselves carefully against all covetousness; for a man’s life does not depend on his possessions, even were he in abundance. […] But God said to him: Fool! this very night your soul will be required of you; and what you have prepared, whose will it be?

<p class="ref">— Luke 12, 15 and 20 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> for we brought nothing into the world, and it is evident that we can carry nothing out of it; if then we have food and clothing, that will suffice us.

<p class="ref">— 1 Timothy 6, 7-8 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> You who do not know what will happen tomorrow! for what is your life? You are a vapor that appears for a little while, and then vanishes.

<p class="ref">— James 4, 14 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

> Set before them the parable of the life of this world. It is like the water that we send down from the sky; the plants of the earth mingle with it; the next day they are dry; the winds scatter them. For God is all-powerful.
>
> Riches and children are the ornaments of the life of this world; but the things that remain, good works, will yield more with your Lord as reward and as hope.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 18, 45-46 [Kazimirski XVIII, 43-44] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> The desire to increase your riches preoccupies you
>
> Until the moment you go down into the grave;
>
> […]
>
> Then you will be questioned about the pleasures of this world.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 102, 1-2 and 8 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> All that is on the earth will pass away.
>
> The face of God alone will remain, surrounded with majesty and glory.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 55, 26-27 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Everything passes: the five traditions repeat it. “All aggregations are transient” (Dhammapada); “All that is on the earth will pass away” (Qur’an); “You are a vapor” (James); pleasure and pain “are not eternal” (Gita). And all draw from it the same lesson: not to be chained to riches, to place one’s **treasure** where it cannot be destroyed — “in heaven,” says the Gospel; in “the things that remain, good works,” says the Qur’an.

#### Where They Differ

Detachment is not flight from the world. The Gita demands that one **act**, renouncing only the fruit of action. Judaism and Islam hold wealth honestly acquired and justly shared to be a blessing. The Gospel goes further in its warning against money. Buddhism, finally, draws from impermanence a conclusion the others do not draw: there is no permanent self to cling to either.

</div>

# Part Six — Destiny

<p class="part-intro">What becomes of our deeds, and what becomes of us? On the law that binds the deed to its fruit, the five voices speak almost in unison. On what awaits man beyond death, they begin to part ways.</p>

## 16. Acts and Their Fruits

Nothing that man does is lost. This conviction runs through the five traditions: good and evil bear their fruits, and no one can escape what he has sown. The agricultural image recurs from one text to the next. But who ripens the harvest? A God who judges, for the religions born of Abraham; an impersonal law, karma, for those of India.

### Hinduism

It is in the Upanishads that one of the earliest statements of the law of karma appears:

> As one acts and as one conducts oneself, so one becomes: the man of good deeds becomes good, the man of evil deeds becomes evil. One becomes pure by pure acts, evil by evil acts. And it is said further: man is made of desires. As is his desire, so is his will; as is his will, so is his act; and whatever act he performs, he will reap its fruit.

<p class="ref">— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad IV, 4, 5 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

> He who acts with hostility toward another (in this life) becomes the victim of similar acts performed by that other. Whatever acts one performs, in whatever body it may be, one must suffer their consequences in those bodies.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata XIII (Anuśasana-parva), section 116 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

And the good accomplished is never lost, even for one who has not attained perfection:

> Son of Pritha, neither here below nor beyond can that man be destroyed: a man of good, my friend, never enters the unhappy way.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita VI, 40 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

> I, the LORD, I search the hearts, I probe the reins; I know how to repay each one according to his ways, according to the fruit of his works.

<p class="ref">— Jeremiah 17, 10 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> For they sow the wind and they reap the whirlwind.

<p class="ref">— Hosea 8, 7 · tr. Zadoc Kahn (excerpt)</p>

Against a proverb that made the children pay for the faults of the fathers, the prophet Ezekiel affirms the responsibility of each one:

> It is the person who sins who shall die: the son shall not bear the fault of the father, nor the father the fault of the son; the righteousness of the righteous is credited to the righteous, the wickedness of the wicked to the wicked.

<p class="ref">— Ezekiel 18, 20 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Consider three things, and you will not fall into the hands of transgression: know what is above you — an eye that sees, an ear that hears, and all your deeds written in a book.

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot 2, 1 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>
### Buddhism

> There is no place in this world, nor in the air, nor in the midst of the ocean, nor in the depths of the mountains, where one may be rid of the evil one has done.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 127 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> One will suffer oneself from an evil deed one has done. By not doing it, one purifies oneself. Pure or impure, each one is so by himself; no one purifies another.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 165 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> Here below, as after his death, in both cases, the good man rejoices. “I have done good,” he says, rejoicing. Greater still is his joy as he advances along the good path.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 18 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

> Be not deceived: God is not mocked. What a man has sown, that he will also reap. He who sows for his flesh will reap corruption from the flesh; but he who sows for the Spirit will reap eternal life from the Spirit.

<p class="ref">— Galatians 6:7-8 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Judge not, that you be not judged. For you will be judged with the judgment with which you judge, and you will be measured with the measure with which you measure.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 7:1-2 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each may receive according to the good or the evil he has done while in his body.

<p class="ref">— 2 Corinthians 5:10 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

> He who has done good of the weight of an atom shall see it,
>
> And he who has committed evil of the weight of an atom shall see it also.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 99:7-8 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> The soul that bears its own burden shall not bear that of another.
>
> Man shall have only what he has earned.
>
> His efforts shall be appraised *at their true worth*,
>
> He shall be rewarded for them with a scrupulous recompense.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 53:38-41 [Kazimirski LIII, 39-42] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

No deed is lost. “What a man has sown, that he will also reap” (Paul); “whatever deed he performs, he will gather its fruit” (Upanishad); good and evil “of the weight of an atom” shall be seen (Qur'an); there is no place in the world where one may “be rid of the evil one has done” (Dhammapada). All likewise insist on **personal responsibility**: no one bears the burden of another (Qur'an), no one purifies another (Dhammapada).

#### Where They Differ

Who brings the harvest to ripeness, and when? For Judaism, Christianity and Islam, it is **God who judges**, at the end of a single life. For Hinduism and Buddhism, it is an **impersonal law**, karma, which operates from life to life. Christianity holds this judgment together with **grace**, which saves beyond all merit; Islam, with the mercy of God; the Gita, with the devotion that delivers “from the bond of works.”

</div>

## 17. Death and What Follows It

No tradition evades death. All ask that one prepare for it, and all affirm that death does not have the last word. But what they hope for beyond it is not the same thing: resurrection and life with God for some, deliverance from the cycle of rebirths for others. It is here that the convergence of the texts reaches its limit, and that the last part of this book begins.

### Hinduism

> As one casts off worn-out garments to take new ones, so the Soul casts off worn-out bodies to put on new bodies.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 22 · tr. Burnouf, 1861</p>

Having come to question Yama, the god of death, about what remains after it, the young Naciketas receives this answer:

> The knowing Self is not born, it does not die; it sprang from nothing, and nothing sprang from it. The Ancient One is unborn, eternal, everlasting; he is not killed when the body is killed.

<p class="ref">— Katha Upanishad I, 2, 18 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

The ultimate goal, however, is not to be reborn, but to be delivered from the cycle:

> As the flowing rivers disappear into the sea, losing their name and their form, so the sage, freed from name and form, goes to the divine Person, greater than the great. He who knows this supreme Brahman becomes Brahman. […] He overcomes sorrow, he overcomes evil; freed from the fetters of the heart, he becomes immortal.

<p class="ref">— Mundaka Upanishad III, 2, 8-9 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

### Judaism

The Hebrew Bible speaks of death with great sobriety:

> let the dust return to the dust, becoming again what it was, and let the spirit go back to God who gave it.

<p class="ref">— Ecclesiastes 12:7 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

One of its latest books nevertheless affirms the resurrection clearly:

> Many of those who sleep in the dust of the ground shall awake, some to everlasting life, others to be an object of shame and everlasting horror.

<p class="ref">— Daniel 12:2 · tr. Zadoc Kahn</p>

The sages of the Mishnah invite us to live this life as a preparation:

> Rabbi Jacob said: This world is like a vestibule before the world to come; prepare yourself in the vestibule, so that you may enter the hall.

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot 4, 16 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>

### Buddhism

> Children, a father, allies are no protection; kinsmen are no protection against the assaults of death.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 288 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> He who looks upon this world with the same eye with which one looks upon a bubble of air, with the same eye with which one looks upon a ray of light, him the king of death (Mara) does not see.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 170 · tr. Hû, 1878</p>

> He who knows that this body is like a light foam, and that it has the consistency of a ray of light, who has broken the flower-tipped arrows of Mara, — he is able to reach the point where he no longer sees the kingdom of death.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 46 · tr. Hû, 1878. Mara personifies death and temptation.</p>

### Christianity

> Jesus said to her: I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me shall live, even though he were dead; and whoever lives and believes in me shall never die. Do you believe this?

<p class="ref">— John 11:25-26 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> Let not your heart be troubled. Believe in God, and believe in me. There are many dwellings in my Father's house. If it were not so, I would have told you. I am going to prepare a place for you.

<p class="ref">— John 14:1-2 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

> He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, and there shall be no more mourning, nor crying, nor pain, for the first things have passed away.

<p class="ref">— Revelation 21:4 · tr. Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

> Every soul shall taste the drink of death. You shall receive your rewards on the day of resurrection. He who has escaped the fire and who enters paradise, that one shall be blessed, for the life of this world is but a deceptive enjoyment.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 3:185 [Kazimirski III, 182] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

The formula every Muslim pronounces on hearing of a death comes from this verse:

> To those who, when a misfortune strikes them, cry out: We belong to God, and to him we shall return.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 2:156 [Kazimirski II, 151] · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> And as for you, O soul of the faithful! Reassured as to your fate,
>
> Return to God, satisfied with your reward, and pleasing to God;
>
> Enter among the number of my servants;
>
> Enter into my paradise.

<p class="ref">— Qur'an 89:27-30 · tr. Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Death is certain and **the same for all**: “every soul shall taste death” (Qur'an); neither children nor kinsmen are “a protection against the assaults of death” (Dhammapada). But no tradition leaves it the last word: “he who believes in me shall live, even though he were dead” (Gospel); the Self “is not killed when the body is killed” (Upanishad); “we belong to God, and to him we shall return” (Qur'an); the sage “no longer sees the kingdom of death” (Dhammapada).

#### Where They Differ

It is here that the paths part most clearly. Christianity and Islam await the **resurrection** and the Judgment, then life with God or far from him; the Hebrew Bible, long reticent about the hereafter, saw the birth of the hope of resurrection that rabbinic Judaism made its own. Hinduism and Buddhism, on the contrary, teach **rebirth**, and place the ultimate goal in the exit from this cycle: union with the Absolute for the one, Nirvana for the other. Part Seven takes up these differences.

</div>

# Part Seven — What Sets Them Apart

<p class="part-intro">The preceding chapters have shown real convergences. It would be dishonest to stop there. On essential questions, the five traditions do not say the same thing, and each holds fast to what makes it unique.</p>

## One God, Several Faces, or No God

This is the deepest difference, and it lies precisely where people often believe the traditions meet.

**Judaism** and **Islam** proclaim a single God, personal, creator, absolutely distinct from his creation, whom no image can represent. The Shema of Israel and the sura of Unity say it with the same force: God is one, he has neither equal nor associate.

**Christianity** also proclaims a single God, but it confesses him in three persons, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. This doctrine of the **Trinity**, formulated at the councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381), is for Christians the very manner in which the one God has revealed himself. For Judaism, it compromises the unity of God; the Qur'an rejects it explicitly.

**Hinduism** holds together what the others separate. It venerates a multitude of deities (Vishnu, Shiva, the Goddess, Ganesh…), but most of its schools understand them as the faces of a single Reality, **Brahman**. Depending on the current, this Reality is conceived as impersonal, beyond all attributes, or as a personal God whom one loves and serves, such as Krishna in the Bhagavad-Gita. The worship of images, which the Bible and the Qur'an forbid, is there a legitimate form of devotion.

**Buddhism**, finally, does not found its path on a creator God. The Buddha refused to speculate on the origin of the world, judging such questions useless for liberation. Gods exist in Buddhist cosmology, but they are themselves beings subject to birth and death. What Buddhism calls the Ultimate, **Nirvana**, is not a person to whom one prays: it is the extinction of craving and of suffering.

To say that “all worship the same God” is therefore inaccurate. One may say, more justly, that all direct man toward a reality that surpasses him and that judges his life. But they do not name it, do not conceive it and do not relate to it in the same way.

## Revelation or Awakening

The five traditions do not give the same authority to their texts, because they do not think of their origin in the same way.

For **Judaism**, the Torah was given by God to Moses; it is the heart of the covenant, and its study is in itself a prayer. For **Islam**, the Qur'an is the uncreated word of God, transmitted word for word in Arabic: a translation is only an interpretation of it, and that is why ritual prayer is performed in Arabic everywhere in the world. For **Christianity**, the supreme revelation is not first of all a book, but a person: Jesus Christ, “the Word made flesh.” The Scriptures are inspired, but written by men, in several languages, and they have been read in translation from the beginning.

For **Hinduism**, the Vedas are not the work of an author, divine or human: they are eternal, and the sages “heard” them. For **Buddhism**, on the other hand, there is no revelation in the proper sense: the Dharma was discovered by a man, through effort and meditation, and the Buddha invites his disciples to accept nothing on the sole strength of his authority, but to test for themselves the truth of what he teaches.

## One Life or Several

The three religions born of Abraham think of time as a line: each human being lives **a single life**, at the end of which comes the judgment of God. Rabbinic Judaism, Christianity and Islam await, at the end of time, the **resurrection of the dead**, each with its own accents.

The two religions born in India think of time as a circle. **Hinduism** and **Buddhism** teach **samsara**, the cycle of rebirths: the being returns in another existence, human or not, according to the weight of its deeds, its **karma**. The ultimate goal is not an eternal life, but the **exit from the cycle**: *moksha*, deliverance, for Hinduism; *Nirvana* for Buddhism.

That is why, when the five traditions affirm with one voice that “each reaps what he has sown,” they do not place the harvest in the same location: on the day of Judgment for some, in a coming life for others.

## The Self: Soul, Atman or Non-Self

Judaism, Christianity and Islam teach that man has a **soul** created by God, personal, destined to endure. The Hinduism of the Upanishads goes further: the deep Self, the **atman**, is eternal, and the non-dualist school of Vedanta teaches that it is identical with Brahman, the Absolute itself — “Thou art That.”

Buddhism here takes the opposite course from all the others. It teaches **anatta**, “non-self”: there is, in the human being, no permanent and independent entity, but a stream of phenomena that arise and pass away. Clinging to a “self” is precisely the root of suffering. This doctrine is one of the distinctive marks of Buddhism, and the reason why it already stood apart, in India, from the Brahmanical schools of its time.

## The Paths of Salvation

All the traditions propose a path; they do not trace the same one.

**Judaism** is first of all a way of living within the covenant: the observance of the commandments of the Torah (tradition counts 613 of them), the Sabbath, study, prayer, justice toward one's neighbor. It cares little for making converts: the “righteous of the nations” who observe the fundamental laws of morality have a share, according to the Talmud, in the world to come.

**Christianity** places **grace** at the center: man is saved not by his works alone, but by faith in Jesus Christ, who died and rose again for him. Good works are the fruit of this faith and not its price. The Churches were long divided over the right articulation of faith and works.

**Islam** requires faith in one God and in his Prophet, and the practice of the **five pillars**: the profession of faith, prayer five times a day, the legal alms, the fast of the month of Ramadan and the pilgrimage to Mecca. On the day of Judgment, God weighs deeds, but his mercy surpasses his wrath.

**Hinduism** recognizes several paths, which the Bhagavad-Gita brings together: the path of **action** performed without attachment to its fruits (*karma-yoga*), the path of **knowledge** (*jnana-yoga*) and the path of loving **devotion** to God (*bhakti-yoga*).

**Buddhism** teaches the **Four Noble Truths** — suffering, its origin in craving, its cessation, and the path that leads there — and this path is the **Noble Eightfold Path**: right understanding, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration. No one can be saved by another: “Set to work, then, with ardor. The Tathagatas are content to preach,” says the Dhammapada (v. 276, tr. Hû) — the Tathagatas being the Buddhas.

## Jesus, Bridge and Stumbling Block

No figure shows better what links and what separates the three Abrahamic religions.

For **Christians**, Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God made man, crucified and risen. For **Judaism**, he is not the awaited Messiah: a Messiah must restore Israel and bring peace to the world, and the world is not at peace. For **Islam**, Jesus (*'Isa*) is one of the greatest prophets, born of the Virgin Mary by the will of God, a worker of miracles, called “Messiah” and “Word of God” by the Qur'an itself; but he is not the Son of God, and the Qur'an affirms that he was neither killed nor crucified, but raised up by God (sura 4, verse 157 in the usual numbering).

The same man is thus, for some, God incarnate, for others a venerated prophet, for others still a Jewish teacher of his time. Hinduism has sometimes welcomed him as a sage or as one divine manifestation among others; Buddhism has no doctrine concerning him.

## The Law, Rites and Boundaries

The moral convergences must not make us forget that each religion is also a community, with its rites, its calendar and its rules.

**Judaism** and **Islam** are religions of the Law: they regulate food (*kashrut*, *halal*), sacred time (the Saturday Sabbath, Friday prayer), ritual purity, family law. **Christianity** very early renounced imposing the ritual law of Moses on non-Jews, and knows hardly any dietary prohibitions, but it has its sacraments, foremost among them baptism and the Eucharist.

**Hinduism** long tied religious duty, *dharma*, to birth and caste. **Buddhism**, from the beginning, contested this link: the Buddha welcomed disciples of every condition and taught that one is not noble by birth, but by deeds.

Finally, the religions do not have the same relationship to other beliefs. Some have sought to be universal and missionary, like Christianity and Islam; others are transmitted first of all by birth, like Judaism and, to a large extent, Hinduism; Buddhism spread throughout Asia by adapting to the cultures it encountered. Their shared history counts centuries of peaceful coexistence, and also wars. The texts quoted in this book have not always been the ones that were remembered.

# Conclusion — Different Lamps

<p class="part-intro">An image, often attributed to the Persian poet Rumi, says that the lamps are different, but that the light is the same.</p>

At the end of this journey, what can honestly be said?

First, that the convergences are not an illusion. They do not stem from a complacent reading that would have sorted the texts to make them agree: they lie at the heart of each of the five traditions, in their oldest and most widely read texts. Whether one opens the Mahabharata, Leviticus, the Dhammapada, the Gospel or the collections of hadiths, one finds there the **Golden Rule**: not to do to others what one would not wish to suffer, to wish for them what one wishes for oneself. One finds there the call to **compassion** toward every living being, the duty to **feed the poor** and to welcome the stranger, the demand to **speak the truth**, the refusal of theft, the struggle against **anger** and **pride**, the price of **forgiveness**. One finds there the idea that what counts is not the appearance of the deed, but the **heart** from which it proceeds, and that our deeds follow us. One finds there, finally, everywhere, the same invitation not to cling to what passes away.

Next, that these convergences lie above all on one precise plane: that of **conduct** and of the **inner life**. It is there that the five voices almost overlap. As soon as one goes back to the ultimate questions — who God is, if he exists; where Scripture comes from; what the soul is; what awaits man after death; how he is saved —, the paths part, and each engages there what is most its own. Part Seven has shown it: one cannot make a Buddhist into a monotheist, nor a Muslim into a Trinitarian, without betraying both.

This finding is in no way disappointing. It says something important: that men and women who share neither the same faith, nor the same idea of God, nor the same hope, can nevertheless **recognize the same good**. It is this intuition that led the Parliament of the World's Religions, in Chicago in 1993, to adopt a *Declaration Toward a Global Ethic*, drafted on the initiative of the theologian Hans Küng and founded precisely on the Golden Rule common to all the traditions. Its principle was simple: there will be no peace among the nations without peace among the religions, and no peace among the religions without dialogue.

There is perhaps a deep reason for this meeting. All these traditions, each in its own way, have looked the same human realities in the face: suffering, death, violence, the desire that is never satisfied, the temptation to believe oneself the center of the world. And all have concluded that man does not find peace by serving himself, but by turning toward something greater than himself — whether God, the Absolute or Awakening — and toward his neighbor.

The believing reader will find his own tradition again in these pages, and perhaps will discover it in a new light on hearing it answer to the traditions of others. The reader who does not believe will at least see that the best of what humanity has thought about how to live together has been entrusted, for three thousand years, to these five great families of texts. And all may hold on to this conviction, which none of the five contradicts: the light one has received does not diminish when it is shared.
