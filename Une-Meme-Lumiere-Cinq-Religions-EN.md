# Foreword

In small towns all over the world, you can find a street where a church and a mosque stand side by side, and perhaps a synagogue, a Hindu temple or a pagoda. The people who go in pray in different languages, read different books and keep different calendars of feasts. Yet if each of them stepped onto the threshold and said aloud what their book asks them to do for their neighbor, you would hear, beneath the different words, almost the same sentence.

This book began with that intuition and set out to test it. It does not summarize what “all religions say,” a convenient formula that often ends up saying nothing. It goes to the Scriptures themselves and looks for the passages where the world’s five great religious traditions meet.

## The Method Followed

**The texts first.** Each chapter rests on quotations, and every quotation was checked against its source before it went in. This book does not report what the Bible, the Qur’an, the Upanishads or the Dhammapada are said to teach; it quotes them, and you can look up every reference.

**Old translations.** This book was first written in French, and it quotes the classic French translations, all in the public domain: the French Rabbinate Bible for the Hebrew Bible, Louis Segond for the New Testament, Kazimirski for the Qur’an, Burnouf for the Bhagavad-Gita, Fernand Hû for the Dhammapada. Some are a century and a half old. This English edition renders each passage from them as closely as possible, without modernizing or embellishing it, because a retouched quotation is no longer a quotation. Each reference names the translation followed, with the word *after* (“after Segond,” “after Kazimirski”). Where no free French translation existed, the passage was translated for this book, and the reference says *working translation*.

**A chronological order.** In each chapter, the five traditions always speak in the same order: **Hinduism**, whose Vedas are the oldest scriptures of the five; **Judaism**, whose Torah is the common root of the three Abrahamic religions; **Buddhism**, born in India in the 5th century BCE; **Christianity**, which appeared in the 1st century; **Islam**, in the 7th. This order is not a ranking; it follows chronology.

**Where they converge, and where they differ.** Each theme ends with a summary box. It sets out what the texts have in common, then, frankly, where they part ways, because to bring together is not to confuse. A Buddhist does not pray to a creator God; a Muslim does not hold Jesus to be the Son of God; a Hindu expects to return in another life, a Jew or a Christian does not. Erasing these differences would show respect to no one. Part Seven is devoted entirely to them.

**Comparing, not leveling.** This book does not claim that all religions are equal, or that deep down they all say the same thing. It asks no one to leave their faith or to water it down. It shows what the texts have in common, then lets each tradition state, in its own words, what it holds true and the others do not: that Jesus is “the way, the truth, and the life,” that the Qur’an is the word of God, that Awakening needs no creator God. Believers will find here a way to understand their neighbors while keeping what they believe.

**The Scriptures first, then the mystics.** The first seventeen themes keep to the Scriptures and their earliest commentaries (the Talmud, the hadiths). The eighteenth is an exception. It gives the floor to the mystics (Kabir, the Kabbalah, Shantideva, *The Cloud of Unknowing*, John of the Cross, Böhme, Ghazali), who claim to have experienced what the Scriptures announce. In the chapter on the Golden Rule, three later or non-canonical texts also extend the Scriptures; they are marked as such.

## How to Read This Book

Part One presents the five traditions, their Scriptures and their founders: what the Torah is, what the Tipitaka contains, how the Qur’an was formed, who Abraham and Moses, the Buddha, Jesus and Muhammad were. It serves as a map before the journey.

The next five parts explore eighteen themes in five areas: the Principle (God, the Absolute, light), the relationship to others (the Golden Rule, compassion, peace, forgiveness, giving, family), conduct (truth, honesty, self-mastery, humility), the inner life (prayer, the heart, detachment) and destiny (deeds and their fruits, death and what follows it, the experience of the mystics). Each theme fits in a few pages and can be read on its own.

Part Seven states what sets the five traditions apart, and the conclusion takes stock of the journey. The appendices set the formulations of the Golden Rule side by side in a table, then give a hearing to a “sixth lamp” from outside the scope of this book, the *Tao Te Ching* of ancient China. The list of sources closes the volume.

# Part One — The Five Traditions and Their Scriptures

<p class="part-intro">Before comparing, we need to know what we are comparing. Each tradition receives, writes down and hands on its sacred texts in its own way. Behind the texts there are also faces: those of the founders and the prophets.</p>

## Hinduism: The Vedas and the Bhagavad-Gita

Hinduism has no founder, no date of birth, no single book. Its faithful readily call it *sanatana dharma*, “the eternal order.” It grew out of the religion of the Indo-Aryan-speaking peoples who settled in northern India in the 2nd millennium BCE, and over three thousand years it has kept growing without breaking with its origins.

Its scriptures fall into two great families.

**The Shruti, “that which was heard.”** These are the texts held to be revealed, which the sages of ancient times, the *rishis*, are said to have perceived rather than composed. First come the four **Vedas**: the *Rig-Veda*, a collection of more than a thousand hymns and the oldest Indian religious text (around 1500-1200 BCE), then the *Sama-Veda*, the *Yajur-Veda* and the *Atharva-Veda*. Attached to each Veda are ritual commentaries and, last of all, the **Upanishads** (from about the 8th century BCE onward), philosophical meditations on the Self (*atman*) and the Absolute (*Brahman*). They are also called *Vedanta*, “the end of the Vedas.”

**The Smriti, “that which is remembered.”** These are the texts of tradition: the laws (including the *Laws of Manu*), the great epics (the *Ramayana* and the *Mahabharata*, the longest poem in the world), the *Puranas* and the manuals of the schools of yoga. At the heart of the *Mahabharata* lies the **Bhagavad-Gita**, the “Song of the Blessed One,” composed between the 5th and the 2nd century BCE: on a battlefield, the god Krishna teaches the warrior Arjuna action without attachment, knowledge and devotion. It is probably the most widely read text in Hinduism.

The sacred language is **Sanskrit**. About **1.2 billion** people identify with Hinduism today, mainly in India and Nepal.

## Judaism: The Written Torah and the Oral Torah

Judaism is the religion of the people of Israel, which traces its covenant with God back to Abraham, and its Law to Moses on Mount Sinai.

Its Bible, the **Hebrew Bible**, is called the *Tanakh*, after the initials of its three parts:

- the **Torah** (“the teaching,” “the Law”): the five books of Moses, from Genesis to Deuteronomy;
- the **Nevi’im**, “the Prophets”: Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings, then Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the twelve minor prophets;
- the **Ketuvim**, “the Writings”: the Psalms, the Proverbs, Job, the Song of Songs, Ruth, the Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther, Daniel, Ezra-Nehemiah and the Chronicles.

Written almost entirely in **Hebrew** (a few chapters of Daniel and Ezra are in Aramaic), it took shape over nearly a millennium. Judaism counts it as twenty-four books; Christians, who received it under the name of the Old Testament, divide it differently.

Alongside the written Torah, Jewish tradition recognizes an **oral Torah**, handed down from master to disciple. It was set down in writing in the **Mishnah**, around the year 200 CE, then commented upon in the **Gemara**; Mishnah and Gemara together form the **Talmud**, which exists in two versions, the Jerusalem Talmud and the Babylonian Talmud (5th-6th centuries). A short tractate of the Mishnah, the **Pirkei Avot** or “Sayings of the Fathers,” gathers the moral maxims of the sages; it is one of the most widely read and most often quoted Jewish texts.

Judaism has about **15 million** adherents. Through its Scriptures, it is the common source of Christianity and Islam.

## Buddhism: The Tipitaka and the Dhammapada

Buddhism was born of the experience of one man: Siddhartha Gautama, a prince of the Shakya clan, in northern India, in the 5th century BCE (his exact dates are debated). Shaken by the sight of suffering, old age and death, he left his palace, searched for years, and under a tree at Bodh-Gaya attained **Awakening** (*bodhi*). He then became the **Buddha**, “the Awakened One,” and taught for forty-five years.

The Buddha wrote nothing. His teaching, the *Dharma*, was recited and memorized by his disciples, then set down in writing in the 1st century BCE, in Ceylon, in the **Pali** language. This canon is called the **Tipitaka**, the “Three Baskets”:

- the **Vinaya**, the discipline of monks and nuns;
- the **Sutta**, the discourses of the Buddha, arranged in five collections;
- the **Abhidhamma**, the systematic analysis of the doctrine.

The fifth collection of discourses contains the **Dhammapada**, “the Path of the Law”: 423 verses divided into 26 chapters, which sum up Buddhist ethics in brief maxims. It is one of the most widely translated Buddhist texts in the world. The Buddhism of the Great Vehicle (*Mahayana*), widespread in China, Tibet, Korea and Japan, has added other scriptures in Sanskrit, Chinese and Tibetan, such as the *Lotus Sutra* or the *Heart Sutra*.

Buddhism does not rest on faith in a creator God. It offers a path of liberation from suffering that each person is invited to test in their own experience. It has about **320 million** adherents, mostly in Asia.

## Christianity: The Old and the New Testament

Christianity was born in the 1st century CE, in Palestine, around **Jesus of Nazareth**, whom his disciples recognized as the Messiah (in Greek *Christos*) foretold by the prophets of Israel, the Son of God who died on the cross and rose again.

The Christian Bible comprises two collections:

- the **Old Testament**, which contains the Jewish Scriptures. Its contents vary from one Church to another: Protestants count 39 books, Catholics 46, the Orthodox Churches more still, and the Ethiopian Church up to 81 books for its Bible as a whole;
- the **New Testament**, 27 books written in **Greek** between about the years 50 and 100: the four **Gospels** (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John), which recount the life and teaching of Jesus; the **Acts of the Apostles**; the **Epistles**, letters of Paul and other apostles to the first communities; and **Revelation**.

The list of recognized books, the *canon*, was settled gradually; the oldest list identical to the present 27 books is that of Athanasius of Alexandria, in 367.

Christianity is now the largest religion in the world, with about **2.3 billion** adherents divided among Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants and the ancient Eastern Churches.

## Islam: The Qur’an and the Sunna

Islam was born in the 7th century in Arabia. According to Muslim tradition, the angel Gabriel (*Jibril*) transmitted the word of God to the prophet **Muhammad**, at Mecca and then at Medina, from the year 610 until his death in 632. *Islam* means “submission” or “surrender of oneself” to God; the *muslim* is the one who surrenders himself to Him.

The **Qur’an** (*al-Qur’an*, “the Recitation”) is held by Muslims to be the very word of God, revealed in **Arabic**. It has **114 suras** (chapters), of very unequal length, arranged roughly from the longest to the shortest, with the exception of the first, the *Fatiha*, “the Opening.” At first memorized and noted down on various materials, it was gathered into a single volume under the caliph Uthman, around 650. The Qur’an presents itself as the confirmation of earlier revelations: it names Abraham, Moses, David, Jesus and Mary, and calls Jews and Christians “the People of the Book.”

Alongside the Qur’an, the **Sunna**, the “tradition” of the Prophet, serves as a guide. It is known through the **hadiths**, accounts of his words and deeds, gathered in the 9th century into great collections, of which the most respected among Sunnis are those of **al-Bukhari** and **Muslim**. In the 13th century, the imam an-Nawawi drew from them forty fundamental hadiths, the *Forty Hadiths*, which many Muslims know by heart.

Islam has about **2 billion** adherents, Sunni for the great majority, Shia for about one in eight.

## Founders, Prophets and Masters

Behind every Scripture there are faces. Judaism recognizes itself in Abraham and Moses, Buddhism in the Buddha, Christianity in Jesus, Islam in Muhammad; Hinduism has no founder, but it awaits a God who comes down among human beings “from age to age.” These figures do not play the same role: among them are messengers, a liberator, an Awakened One, the Son of God, a divine incarnation. Listening to how each tradition speaks of its own is already a way into what sets it apart.

### Hinduism

Hinduism goes back to no founder. It attributes its Vedas to the *rishis*, the seers of ancient times, and holds the sage Vyasa to be the compiler of the Vedas and the author of the Mahabharata. But the Bhagavad-Gita lets another voice be heard: that of Krishna, in whom Hindus recognize an *avatara*, a “descent” of the god Vishnu. He himself says why he is born among men:

> When justice languishes, Bharata, when injustice rises again, then I make myself a creature, and I am born from age to age. For the defense of the good, for the ruin of the wicked, for the restoration of justice.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita IV, 7-8 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

Rama, the hero of the Ramayana, is another of these avatars; and great masters, such as Shankara in the 8th century, gave the tradition its schools of thought without ever claiming to found it.

### Judaism

With Abraham, everything begins with a departure. The man whom Jews, Christians and Muslims call their father in faith leaves his land in answer to a call:

> The LORD had said to Abram: Go away from your country, from your birthplace and from your father’s house, to the land that I will show you. I will make you a great nation; I will bless you, I will make your name glorious, and you shall be a pattern of blessing. I will bless those who bless you, and whoever insults you I will curse; and through you all the families of the earth shall be happy.

<p class="ref">— Genesis 12:1-3 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

Moses, a few centuries later according to the biblical account, receives a mission of liberation. At the burning bush, God presents himself to him as the one who hears the oppressed:

> The LORD continued: “I have seen, I have seen the humiliation of my people who are in Egypt; I have heard their cry against their oppressors, for I know their sufferings. So I have intervened to deliver them from the power of Egypt […].”

<p class="ref">— Exodus 3:7-8 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

Moses receives the Torah at Sinai, and the Bible places him above all the prophets who follow, from Elijah to Isaiah and Jeremiah:

> But there has not arisen again in Israel a prophet like Moses, with whom the Lord had communicated face to face […].

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 34:10 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

Buddhism was born of the experience of one man, Siddhartha Gautama. The Dhammapada has kept the words that tradition attributes to him at the moment of Awakening, under the tree at Bodh-Gaya:

> I have traveled, without finding anything, through a cycle of many rebirths, in search of the Builder of the house… Painful is a continual return to life! But, Builder of the house, I know you now! you will not build it again. Broken are all the ties (of your rafters), shattered too your ridgepole! […] my mind has reached the total extinction of desire.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 153-154 · after Hû, 1878. For the tradition, the “builder” is craving (<em>tanha</em>), which endlessly rebuilds the house of existence; Hû sees in it Mara.</p>

At Benares, he then gives his first teaching to five ascetics who had shared his years of mortification: a middle way between the life of pleasure and the asceticism that tortures the body.

> There are two extremes, O Bhikkhus, which the man who has given up the world ought not to follow — the habitual practice, on the one hand, of those things whose attraction depends upon the passions, and especially of sensuality — a low and pagan way (of seeking satisfaction) unworthy, unprofitable […] — and the habitual practice, on the other hand, of asceticism (or self-mortification), which is painful, unworthy, and unprofitable. There is a middle path, O Bhikkhus, avoiding these two extremes, discovered by the Tathagata — a path which opens the eyes, and bestows understanding, which leads to peace of mind, to the higher wisdom, to full enlightenment, to Nirvana!

<p class="ref">— Sermon at Benares (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta) 2-3 · tr. Rhys Davids, 1881</p>

The Buddha presents himself as neither a god nor a prophet: he is the one who found the path, and who shows it.

### Christianity

Christianity rests on a person rather than on a book or a law: Jesus of Nazareth, a Jew from Galilee, who taught for a few years (three, according to the Gospel of John) before being crucified in Jerusalem, around the year 30, under the Roman governor Pontius Pilate. In the synagogue of Nazareth, he opens the book of Isaiah and makes it the program of his mission:

> The Spirit of the Lord is upon me,
>
> Because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor;
>
> He has sent me to heal the brokenhearted,
>
> To proclaim deliverance to the captives,
>
> And recovery of sight to the blind,
>
> To set the oppressed free,
>
> To proclaim a year of the Lord’s grace.

<p class="ref">— Luke 4:18-19 · after Segond, 1910</p>

His disciples, and then the apostle Paul, carried this message as far as Rome. For them, Jesus is more than a prophet: he is the Christ, the Son of God, who died and rose again.

### Islam

Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570. Orphaned very young, he became a caravan merchant known for his integrity, and he was about forty when, according to tradition, the angel Gabriel appeared to him in a cave on Mount Hira, in 610. These are said to be the first verses revealed:

> Read, in the name of your Lord who created all,
>
> Who created man of <span class="sc">clotted blood</span>?
>
> Read, for your Lord is the most generous,
>
> It is he who taught (man) to use the pen (the kalem);
>
> He taught man what man did not know.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 96:1-5 · after Kazimirski, 1869. “Clotted blood” is the title of the sura.</p>

The Qur’an does not make him a divine being. He is “the envoy of God,” the last of a long chain of prophets:

> Mohammed is not the father of any man among you. He is the envoy of God and the seal of the prophets. God knows all things.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 33:40 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

Islam traces this chain back to Abraham, and includes Moses and Jesus in it:

> Say: We believe in God, in what he has sent down to us, in what he revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Jacob and the twelve tribes; we believe in the holy books that Moses, Jesus and the prophets received from heaven; we make no distinction between them, we are resigned to the will of God (*we are Muslims*).

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 3:84 [Kazimirski III, 78] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

Muhammad died in Medina in 632, having gathered Arabia around the new faith.

#### Where They Converge

Each tradition traces its origin to a decisive encounter: the call of Abraham, Moses at the burning bush, the Awakening under the tree at Bodh-Gaya, the reading of Isaiah in Nazareth, the cave on Mount Hira, or, for Hinduism, the word of Krishna on the battlefield. And each sees in it the beginning of a mission turned toward others: “through you all the families of the earth shall be happy” (Genesis), “to bring good news to the poor” (Luke), born “for the defense of the good” (Gita), teaching the middle path to five companions (Sermon at Benares). Three of them also recognize a common ancestor: Abraham, whom the Bible and the Qur’an call the friend of God.

#### Where They Differ

The status of these figures changes everything from one tradition to the next. Moses and Muhammad are prophets, men to whom God speaks, and the Qur’an insists on it: Muhammad is only “the envoy of God.” The Buddha is neither a god nor a prophet: he found the path by himself. Krishna is a god who becomes a creature, in a tradition that knows no founder. Jesus, finally, is for Christians far more than a prophet: the Son of God made man. On him the three religions born of Abraham part ways, as Part Seven will show.

# Part Two — The Principle

<p class="part-intro">Where everything comes from, and where it is going. Before telling us how to live, each tradition speaks of what lies beyond us, and almost all of them reach for the same image to say it: light.</p>

## 1. The Ultimate: God, the Absolute, Nirvana

None of the five traditions makes the human being the measure of all things. All of them point to a first reality, unborn and undying, that gives meaning to everything else. Yet this is where their words differ most. Some call it God and pray to it as Father or Lord; Hinduism names it Brahman and recognizes it under a thousand faces; Buddhism refuses to see it as a creator and describes it by what it is not. So each voice deserves a careful hearing, without being lent the words of another.

### Hinduism

The oldest religious text of India already affirms, beneath the multitude of divine names, the unity of a single being:

> The divine spirit that moves in the sky is called Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni. The sages give the one being more than one name: it is Agni, Yama, Matarishvan.

<p class="ref">— Rig-Veda I, 164, 46 · after Langlois, 1848-1851</p>

The Upanishads go further: this one Reality, Brahman, is also the innermost Self of every being. The sage Uddalaka teaches it to his son:

> That subtle essence (the root of all), in it everything that exists has its Self. It is the True. It is the Self, and you, O Śvetaketu, are That.

<p class="ref">— Chandogya Upanishad VI, 8, 7 · working translation after Müller, 1879</p>

In the Bhagavad-Gita, the Absolute reveals itself as a personal God, Krishna, who shows the warrior Arjuna his “universal form”:

> There then, in the body of the God of gods, the son of Pandu saw the whole Universe, One in its multiplicity.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XI, 13 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

The Shema, which the Jew recites morning and evening, proclaims the unity of God and at once draws from it a demand for love:

> Hear, O Israel: the LORD is our God, the LORD is one! You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul and with all your might.

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 6:4-5 · after Zadoc Kahn, French Rabbinate Bible, 1899-1906</p>

> Acknowledge now, and impress it upon your heart, that the LORD alone is God, in heaven above as here below on earth, and that there is no other!

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 4:39 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Thus says the LORD, king and redeemer of Israel, the LORD-Cebaot: “I am the first, I am the last, apart from me there is no God!”

<p class="ref">— Isaiah 44:6 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

The Buddha does not speak of a God. Yet in one of his “solemn utterances” he affirms that a reality exists beyond all becoming, and that this reality makes deliverance possible:

> “There is, O Bhikkhus, an unborn, unoriginated, uncreated, unformed. If there were not, O Bhikkhus, this unborn, unoriginated, uncreated, unformed, there would be no way out of the world of the born, the originated, the created, the formed. Since, O Bhikkhus, there is an unborn, unoriginated, uncreated, unformed, there is therefore a way out of the born, the originated, the created, the formed.”

<p class="ref">— Udana VIII, 3 · working translation after Strong, 1902</p>

This “unborn” bears a name: Nirvana, which the Dhammapada places above all the goods of this world.

> Health is the best of acquisitions; contentment, the best of riches; trust, the best of kin; Nirvana, the supreme happiness.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 204 · after Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

Asked about the first of the commandments, Jesus answers by quoting the profession of faith of Israel:

> Jesus answered: This is the first: Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God is the one Lord; and: You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind, and with all your strength.

<p class="ref">— Mark 12:29-30 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> God is Spirit, and those who worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.

<p class="ref">— John 4:24 · after Segond, 1910</p>

The first letter of John adds the definition that sums up the whole of the Christian faith:

> Beloved, let us love one another; for love is of God, and whoever loves is born of God and knows God. He who does not love has not known God, for God is love.

<p class="ref">— 1 John 4:7-8 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The sura of Unity, one of the shortest in the Qur’an, is held by tradition to be the summary of the whole faith:

> Say: God is one.
>
> He is the God to whom all beings turn in their needs.
>
> He has not begotten, and was not begotten.
>
> He has no equal in anyone whatsoever.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 112:1-4 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> God is the only God; there is no other God but him, the Living, the Unchanging. Neither slumber nor sleep has any hold on him. All that is in the heavens and on the earth belongs to him. […] His throne extends over the heavens and the earth, and their keeping costs him no effort. He is the Most High, the Great.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2:255, the “Throne Verse” [Kazimirski II, 256] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Nothing resembles him; he hears and sees all.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 42:11 [Kazimirski XLII, 9] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

The five traditions direct man toward a reality which neither is born nor dies and which escapes all representation. The Shema of Israel, taken up by Jesus, and the sura of Unity proclaim that it is one; the Rig-Veda sees “the one being” beneath all the divine names; the Buddha affirms an “unborn, uncreated.” All also say that it surpasses images: “Nothing resembles him,” says the Qur’an; “God is Spirit,” says the Gospel; Nirvana is “unformed.”

#### Where They Differ

The words part company at once. For Judaism, Christianity and Islam, this reality is a **personal God** and creator, whom Christianity confesses in three persons, a belief the other two reject. Hinduism sees it sometimes as an impersonal Absolute, Brahman, identical with the Self of each person (“you are That”), sometimes as a God to be loved, Krishna. Buddhism posits **no creator God**: the “unborn” is no one to pray to; it is the way out of suffering. Part Seven returns to this difference, the one with the heaviest consequences.

</div>

## 2. Light

To speak of this reality beyond everything, the five traditions often reach for the same image. Every tradition speaks of light. The word stands in turn for the divine itself, for the word that guides, for the awakening of the mind, for the radiance of the righteous. It is the most universal image of the sacred, and it gave this book its title.

### Hinduism

The Upanishads place this light beyond all visible lights:

> In the highest golden sheath is Brahman, without passions and without parts. It is pure, it is the light of lights […]. There shine neither the sun, nor the moon and the stars, nor these lightnings, and still less this fire. When He shines, everything shines after him; by his light all this is illumined.

<p class="ref">— Mundaka Upanishad II, 2, 9-10 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

When Krishna unveils his divine form, the narrator finds no other comparison:

> If in the sky there rose all at once the Light of a thousand suns, it would be comparable to the splendor of that magnanimous God.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XI, 12 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Light of luminous bodies, he is beyond darkness. Knowledge, object of knowledge, goal of knowledge, he is in the depths of every heart.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XIII, 17 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

Where India sees a light beyond the world, the Hebrew Bible prays to a God who gives light and protection to those who trust in him:

> Of David. The Lord is my light and my salvation: whom should I fear? The Lord is the rampart that protects my life: whom should I dread?

<p class="ref">— Psalm 27:1 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Torah itself is light for the one who follows it:

> Your word is a torch that lights my steps, a light that shines upon my path.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 119:105 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

And God has set a lamp within man:

> The soul of man is a divine torch, which carries its gleams into the recesses of the heart.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 20:27 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> I, the LORD, have called you for righteousness and I take you by the hand; I protect you and I establish you as the federation of the peoples and the light of the nations.

<p class="ref">— Isaiah 42:6 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

In the evening of his life, the Buddha refuses to let his disciples lean on any outside authority, even his own:

> Therefore, O Ananda, be lamps unto yourselves. Be a refuge unto yourselves. Seek no outside refuge. Hold fast to the truth as a lamp. Hold fast to the truth as a refuge. Seek refuge in no one other than yourselves.

<p class="ref">— Mahaparinibbana Sutta II, 33 · working translation after Rhys Davids, 1881</p>

> By day shines the sun; by night shines the moon; armed from head to foot, shines the warrior; in meditation, shines the Brahmana. But by day as by night, with unbroken radiance, shines the Buddha.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 387 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> He who, at first heedless, afterward ceases to be so, he spreads in this world a radiance like that of the moon freed from clouds.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 172 · after Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

The New Testament takes up the image and makes it a definition of God:

> The message we have heard from him, and which we declare to you, is that God is light, and that in him there is no darkness.

<p class="ref">— 1 John 1:5 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Jesus spoke to them again, and said: I am the light of the world; he who follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life.

<p class="ref">— John 8:12 · after Segond, 1910</p>

And the light received must in turn enlighten others:

> You are the light of the world. A city set on a mountain cannot be hidden; and one does not light a lamp to put it under the bushel, but one puts it on the lampstand, and it gives light to all who are in the house. Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5:14-16 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The “Verse of Light” is one of the most meditated-upon texts of Muslim mysticism:

> God is the <span class="sc">light</span> of the heavens and of the earth. This light is like a niche in which there is a lamp, a lamp placed in a crystal, a crystal like a brilliant star; this lamp is lit with the oil of a blessed tree, an olive tree that is neither of the East nor of the West, and whose oil shines even though fire does not touch it. It is light upon light. God leads to his light whom he will, and sets forth parables for men; for he knows all things.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 24:35 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Light has come to you from God, as well as this clear Book by which God will guide those who follow his will into the paths of salvation. He will bring them out of darkness into light by his will, and will direct them on the straight path.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 5:15-16 [Kazimirski V, 18] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Everywhere, the Ultimate is light, and a light **beyond all lights**: “there shine neither the sun nor the moon,” say the Upanishads; “God is light, and in him there is no darkness,” says the apostle John; “God is the light of the heavens and of the earth,” says the Qur’an. And everywhere this light passes to human beings: it is “in the depths of every heart” in the Gita, the disciples of Jesus become “the light of the world,” he who awakens “spreads a radiance like that of the moon” in the Dhammapada.

#### Where They Differ

Where does the light come from? For the religions born of Abraham, it comes from God and guides through revelation: the Law, the Gospel, the “clear Book.” Christianity goes further: Jesus identifies himself with it in person. Hinduism discovers it within, in the Self. Buddhism refuses any outside light: “be lamps unto yourselves.”

</div>

# Part Three — The Other

<p class="part-intro">Here the five voices come closest together. Who is my neighbor? What do I owe him? How should I answer the harm he does me? On these questions, texts written centuries and thousands of miles apart sometimes seem to answer one another word for word.</p>

## 3. The Golden Rule

The Scriptures do not speak of God only to stay in heaven: they draw from it at once a way of behaving toward others, and its first rule is almost everywhere the same. The “Golden Rule” asks us to treat others as we would wish to be treated. It comes in two forms: one negative (“do not do to others what you would not want done to you”), the other positive (“do for others what you would want done for you”). No other moral maxim is so widely shared: all five traditions know it, and several present it as the summary of their whole Law.

### Hinduism

In the thirteenth book of the Mahabharata, the great epic of India, the Golden Rule is given as the summary of *dharma*, the just order:

> He who regards all creatures as himself, and behaves toward them as toward himself […] attains happiness. […] Let one never do to another what one holds to be harmful to oneself. That, in brief, is the rule of Righteousness. […] When one harms another, the one who has suffered the wrong turns and harms in his turn; likewise, when one cherishes another, the other cherishes in his turn. Let one govern one’s conduct accordingly.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata XIII (Anushasana-parva), section 113 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

One of the most popular collections of fables in India, the *Hitopadesa* (around the 12th century), quotes the law books in its turn. It does so with some irony, putting these maxims in the mouth of an old tiger who wants to lure a traveler. The maxims themselves are serious, though, and they state the rule both ways:

> As life is dear to one’s self, so also are those of all beings. On account of the resemblance to themselves, the good exercise compassion towards all living beings. […] Both in refusing and in giving; in pleasure and in pain; in what is agreeable and disagreeable; a man obtains a sure standard by a comparison with himself.

<p class="ref">— Hitopadesa I, the tiger and the traveler · tr. Johnson, 1848</p>

### Judaism

The oldest written form of this commandment appears in the Torah; Jesus would later call it the “second” commandment:

> You shall not take vengeance nor bear a grudge against the children of your people, but love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19:18 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

A few verses further on, the same chapter extends this “as yourself” to the stranger:

> He shall be to you as one of your countrymen, the stranger who sojourns with you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt I am the LORD your God.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19:34 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

In the 1st century BCE, the sage Hillel made it the summary of the whole Law, in a famous story:

> Once a Gentile came to Shammai, and said: “Make me a proselyte, but on condition that you teach me the whole law, yes, the whole of it, while I stand on one leg.” Shammai drove him away with the builder’s rule he held in his hand. When he came to Hillel with the same challenge, Hillel converted him by answering him on the spot: “What is hateful to yourself, do not do to your neighbor. This is the whole law, and the rest is its commentary” […].

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a · working translation after Hershon, *A Talmudic Miscellany*, 1880. The Aramaic adds: “go, study.”</p>

An apocryphal writing preserved only in Old Slavonic, which many scholars date to the 1st century CE and regard as Jewish, gives a formula close to it:

> And now, my children, keep your hearts from every injustice, which the Lord hates. Just as a man asks something for his own soul from God, so let him do to every living soul […].

<p class="ref">— Book of the Secrets of Enoch (2 Enoch) 61:1 · tr. Morfill, 1896. An apocryphal writing, received in no canon.</p>

Seventeen centuries later, a scholar from Vilna, Pinchas Eliyahu Horowitz, asks who this “neighbor” of Leviticus is. His answer, in the *Sefer HaBrit* (1797), one of the most widely read Hebrew books of its time, leaves no people outside:

> The essence of the love of one’s neighbor consists in this: that a man love the whole human race, of whatever people and of whatever language it may be, because each is a man, made in the likeness and the image as he himself is […]. For man was not created for himself alone: every man exists also for another man […].

<p class="ref">— Pinchas Eliyahu Horowitz, *Sefer HaBrit* II, 13, chap. 1 · working translation from the Hebrew (Brünn, 1797)</p>

> […] the intention here is not Israel alone, for if it were so, it would have been written “You shall love your brother as yourself” […]. All the nations are included, and every man is a neighbor to man.

<p class="ref">— *Sefer HaBrit* II, 13, chap. 5 · working translation from the Hebrew</p>

### Buddhism

The Dhammapada bases the ban on harming others on a simple look at oneself: everyone fears for their own life, so everyone must spare the lives of others.

> Everyone trembles before violence, everyone trembles before death. Let each do what he would wish another to do; let him not kill; let him not cause to kill.
>
> Everyone trembles before violence; to everyone life is dear. Let each do what he would wish another to do; let him not kill, let him not cause to kill.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 129-130 · after Hû, 1878</p>

The phrase “let each do what he would wish another to do” is an interpretation by the French translator; Max Müller’s English translation (1881) renders the same passage as “remember that you are like unto them.” So the text first invites us to recognize ourselves in others, and from there forbids us to harm them. A parallel collection, the Udanavarga, states the rule explicitly:

> Wherever one looks, there is nothing dearer to a man than himself; therefore, since it is the same thing that is dear to you and dear to others, do not wound others with what makes you suffer yourself.

<p class="ref">— Udanavarga V, 18 · working translation after Rockhill, 1883</p>

In the 8th century, the monk Shantideva, in a poem that Buddhists of the Great Vehicle still meditate upon, draws from it the most direct consequence:

> What joy is to me, it is to others; what pain is to me, it is to others. I must do for others what I do for myself. […] I must destroy the pain of my neighbor, because it is pain, as my own pain is; I must serve my neighbor, because he is a living being as I myself am. Self and neighbor aspire equally to happiness: what special character does the self present, that we should put our efforts at its service alone?

<p class="ref">— Shantideva, *Bodhicaryavatara* VIII, 90 and 94-95 · after La Vallée Poussin, 1907</p>

### Christianity

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus gives the Golden Rule in its positive form, and makes it the summary of all Scripture:

> All things whatsoever you would that men should do to you, do you even so to them, for this is the law and the prophets.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 7:12 · after Segond, 1910</p>

The Gospel of Luke places it at the heart of the teaching on love of enemies, which extends it beyond simple reciprocity:

> But I say to you who listen: Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who mistreat you. […] What you want men to do for you, do likewise for them.

<p class="ref">— Luke 6:27-28 and 31 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Golden Rule of Islam is formulated in a hadith that the imam an-Nawawi kept among the forty fundamental sayings of the Prophet:

> “None of you [truly] believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 13) and Muslim (no. 45) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

In one of the versions reported by Muslim, the narrator hesitates between two words, and here “neighbor” comes before “brother”:

> “By Him who holds my soul in His hand, no servant [truly] believes until he loves for his neighbor — or he said: for his brother — what he loves for himself.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by Muslim (no. 45) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

This is the clearest convergence in the book. All five traditions know the Golden Rule, and several make it the **summary of their Law**: “this is the law and the prophets,” says Jesus; “this, in brief, is the rule of Righteousness,” says the Mahabharata; “none of you truly believes” without it, says the hadith; “I must do for others what I do for myself,” writes Shantideva. The Torah had laid it down first: “love your neighbor as yourself” (Leviticus 19:18); the sage Hillel gave it as “the whole law,” of which “the rest is commentary.” In every case it rests on the same gesture: **putting oneself in the place of the other**.

#### Where They Differ

The form varies: negative (“do not do to others…”) in the Mahabharata, in Hillel and in the Udanavarga, positive (“do to them…”) in the Gospel, the hadith, the Book of the Secrets of Enoch and Shantideva; the Hitopadesa makes it a rule of comparison that holds for refusing as well as for giving. The foundation varies as well: the identity of a single Self in all beings for Hinduism, the shared experience of fear and suffering for Buddhism, the love of God and neighbor for Judaism and Christianity, faith for Islam. And the circle is not equally wide everywhere: “all creatures” in India, the “neighbor” in the Bible, the “brother” or the “neighbor” in the hadith. Jewish tradition also debated who this “neighbor” is: in 1797, the Sefer HaBrit includes in it “the whole human race,” except for two kinds of men: those who live outside any society, and those who destroy the world, murderers and robbers.

</div>

## 4. Compassion and Mercy

The Golden Rule says what is just; compassion goes further. It is the movement of the heart that makes us suffer at another’s suffering and moves us to relieve it. The Abrahamic religions see it first in God, “the Merciful,” and ask us to resemble Him. The traditions of India make it a disposition to be cultivated toward all living beings.

### Hinduism

The Bhagavad-Gita draws the portrait of the man whom God loves:

> The man without hatred for any living thing, kind and merciful, without selfishness, without self-regard, equal in pleasure and in pain, patient; Joyful, always in a state of Union, master of himself, firm in good purpose, his mind and reason fixed on me, my servant: that man is dear to me.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XII, 13-14 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

The Upanishads give the deep reason for this benevolence: he who sees the same Self in all can no longer hate anything.

> And he who beholds all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, never turns away from it. When, for the man who understands, the Self has become all things, what sorrow, what trouble can there be for him who has once beheld that oneness?

<p class="ref">— Isha Upanishad 6-7 · working translation after Müller, 1879</p>

### Judaism

When God reveals Himself to Moses on Sinai, He proclaims His mercy first:

> The Divinity passed before him and proclaimed: “ADONAI is the eternal Being, all-powerful, gracious, merciful, slow to anger, full of benevolence and equity; He keeps His favor to the thousandth generation; He bears with crime, rebellion, fault, but He does not absolve them […].”

<p class="ref">— Exodus 34:6-7 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Gracious and merciful is the LORD, slow to anger and abounding in grace. The LORD is good to all, His pity extends to all His creatures.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 145:8-9 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud dares a striking image: God Himself prays that His mercy may prevail over His rigor.

> Thus does He pray: “May it please Me that My mercy prevail over My anger, that all My attributes be clothed in compassion, and that I deal with My children according to the attribute of goodness, and that for their sake I pass beyond judgment.”

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 7a · working translation after Hershon, 1880</p>

### Buddhism

The Metta Sutta, one of the most recited texts of early Buddhism, describes boundless goodwill through the simplest of images:

> As a mother, at the risk of her life, watches over her own child, her only child, so let each cultivate toward all beings a boundless (friendly) mind. And let him cultivate benevolence toward the whole world, a boundless (friendly) mind, above, below and across, without obstacle, without hatred, without enmity. Standing, walking, sitting or lying down, as long as he is awake, let him devote himself to this state of mind; this (way of) living, it is said, is the best in this world.

<p class="ref">— Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipata 148-150 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

> It is by gentleness that anger must be conquered; by good that evil must be conquered; by liberality, avarice; by truth, falsehood.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 223 · after Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

In the Gospel of Matthew, Jesus links the love of God with the love of neighbor, which he takes from Leviticus:

> Jesus answered him: You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind. This is the first and greatest commandment. And here is the second, which is like it: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 22:37-40 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Be therefore merciful, as your Father is merciful.

<p class="ref">— Luke 6:36 · after Segond, 1910</p>

To the question “who is my neighbor?”, Jesus answers with the parable of the Good Samaritan, in which the man who helps the wounded traveler is a despised foreigner:

> But a Samaritan, who was traveling, having come there, was moved with compassion when he saw him. He went to him, and bound up his wounds, pouring oil and wine on them […]. Which of these three seems to you to have been the neighbor of the one who fell among the robbers? It is the one who showed mercy to him, answered the doctor of the law. And Jesus said to him: Go, and you, do likewise.

<p class="ref">— Luke 10:33-37 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

All the suras of the Qur’an, save one, open with the same formula, which names a single attribute of God, His mercy:

> *In the name of the gracious and merciful God*.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 1:1, the basmala · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> […] my mercy embraces all things; I destine it for those who fear, who give alms and who believe in my signs […].

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 7:156 [Kazimirski VII, 155] · after Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

> We have sent you, *O Muhammad!* only as a mercy for the universe.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 21:107 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

And the kindness owed to men extends in circles, from parents to the stranger:

> Worship God and associate nothing with Him. Show kindness to your fathers and mothers, to your kin, to orphans, to the poor, to the clients bound to you by blood and to foreign clients, to your companions, to travelers and to your slaves. God does not love the presumptuous and boastful man.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 4:36 [Kazimirski IV, 40] · after Kazimirski, 1869. The “clients” are, in Arabic, the neighbors, near or distant.</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

All the traditions put compassion at the top of the virtues. The Abrahamic religions contemplate it **in God** and ask man to imitate it: “Be merciful, as your Father is merciful”; the Qur’an opens every sura with divine mercy, and the Hebrew Bible proclaims a God “gracious and merciful” whose “pity extends to all His creatures.” The traditions of India cultivate it as a disposition **toward all beings**, and the Metta Sutta describes it through the universal image of the mother watching over her only child.

#### Where They Differ

In Judaism, Christianity and Islam, human compassion answers that of God; the Gospel expressly extends it to the stranger and the enemy. In Hinduism, it flows from the vision of the same Self in all; in Buddhism, it is a state of mind that one develops through meditation, without reference to a God. The Indian traditions explicitly extend it to animals.

</div>

## 5. Non-Violence and Peace

Compassion gives rise to the refusal of violence. All the traditions condemn murder and praise peace, but they draw different conclusions: some refuse all violence, others regulate the use of force. The texts below state the ideal; the summary also notes its limits.

### Hinduism

Non-violence, *ahimsa*, is one of the highest virtues of Hinduism. The Gita counts it among the qualities of the man “born to a divine condition”; Burnouf renders the word as “the peaceable temper”:

> The peaceable temper, truthfulness, gentleness, renunciation, inner calm, benevolence, pity for living beings, peace of heart, mildness, modesty, gravity;

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVI, 2 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Abstention from cruelty is the highest Religion. Abstention from cruelty is the highest self-mastery. Abstention from cruelty is the highest gift. Abstention from cruelty is the highest penance. Abstention from cruelty is the highest sacrifice. […] He who abstains from cruelty is the father and mother of all creatures.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata XIII (Anushasana-parva), section 116 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

The Yoga-sutras describe its fruit:

> As soon as he is firmly established in abstention from harming, his presence causes enmity to cease.

<p class="ref">— Yoga-sutra of Patanjali II, 35 · working translation after Woods, 1914</p>

### Judaism

The Decalogue states the prohibition in one brief sentence:

> You shall not commit homicide.

<p class="ref">— Exodus 20:13 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Mishnah draws from the creation of a single man, Adam, the infinite value of each life:

> This is why God created Adam alone […], to show us that he who saves a single human being saves a whole world, and that he who destroys a man is to be likened to one who destroys a whole world. The fact that God created a single man also had the purpose of showing that all men are brothers, and of preventing anyone from being able to believe himself superior to an individual of another nation […].

<p class="ref">— Mishnah, Sanhedrin 4:5 · after Moïse Schwab, 1878-1890. Schwab follows the universal form of the text; the printed editions of the Hebrew read “a single soul of Israel.”</p>

The prophets announce the end of war:

> […] they then shall forge from their swords plowshares and from their spears pruning hooks; one people shall no longer draw the sword against another people, and the art of war shall no longer be learned.

<p class="ref">— Isaiah 2:4 · after Zadoc Kahn (excerpt)</p>

> Hillel said: Be of the disciples of Aaron, loving peace and pursuing peace, loving creatures and bringing them near to the Torah.

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot (Sayings of the Fathers) 1:12 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>

### Buddhism

The first of the five precepts that the Buddha gives the layman concerns the life of every being:

> Let him not kill, nor cause to kill any living being, and let him not approve of others killing, having refrained from doing harm to all creatures, both those that are strong and those that tremble in the world.

<p class="ref">— Dhammika Sutta, Sutta Nipata 393 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

> Victory breeds hatred, for the vanquished feels pain. He who lives in peace is happy, no longer thinking of victory or of defeat.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 201 · after Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

Jesus makes peace a beatitude, and goes as far as asking us to love our enemies:

> Blessed are those who bring about peace, for they shall be called sons of God!

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5:9 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> You have heard that it was said: an eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth. But I say to you, do not resist the wicked one. If someone strikes you on the right cheek, offer him the other also. […] You have heard that it was said: You shall love your neighbor, and you shall hate your enemy. But I say to you: Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and who persecute you, […]

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5:38-39 and 43-44 · after Segond, 1910</p>

At the moment of his arrest, Jesus refuses to be defended by the sword:

> Then Jesus said to him: Put your sword back in its place; for all those who take the sword shall perish by the sword.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 26:52 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Do not let yourself be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good.

<p class="ref">— Romans 12:21 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an takes up a law that it says was given to the children of Israel, and which makes every life the equal of all humanity:

> He who has killed a man, without that man having killed a man or sown disorder in the land, shall be regarded as the murderer of the human race; and he who has restored life to a man shall be regarded as if he had restored life to the whole human race.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 5:32 [Kazimirski V, 35] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> No compulsion in religion.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2:256 [Kazimirski II, 257] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Evil and good cannot walk together. Render good for evil, and you will see your enemy change into a protector and a friend.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 41:34 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> If they incline to peace, you shall incline to it also, and you shall put your trust in God, for He hears and knows all.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 8:61 [Kazimirski VIII, 63] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

All affirm the **sacredness of life** and condemn murder. The Mishnah teaches that “he who saves a single human being saves a whole world,” and the Qur’an takes up this law, saying it was given to the children of Israel; the Mahabharata makes abstention from cruelty “the highest Religion”; the first Buddhist precept protects every living being. All exalt peace, and several ask us to **render good for evil**: “Render good for evil” (Qur’an), “overcome evil with good” (Paul); “what makes hatreds cease here below is by no means hatred” (Dhammapada 5).

#### Where They Differ

The ideal is shared; the relationship to force is not. Buddhism is the most radical. Hinduism makes *ahimsa* a major virtue, yet the Gita is set on a battlefield, where Krishna reminds the warrior of his duty to fight. Jesus commands the love of enemies, but the Churches developed a doctrine of “just war.” The Hebrew Bible knows the wars of Israel alongside the prophetic hope of a universal peace. The Qur’an, finally, authorizes defensive combat and regulates it (2:190; 22:39): its demand for peace is not an absolute pacifism.

</div>

## 6. Forgiveness

Peace asks for more than the absence of violence: it asks us to know how to forgive. Answering offense with offense keeps hatred going forever. The five traditions know this, and all of them invite us to break the cycle. They disagree, though, about where forgiveness comes from: a gift of God to be imitated for some, an inner liberation from resentment for others.

### Hinduism

Exiled and humiliated, King Yudhishthira refuses to yield to the anger his wife Draupadi urges upon him, and answers her with a eulogy of forgiveness:

> Forgiveness is virtue; forgiveness is sacrifice; forgiveness is the Vedas, forgiveness is Revelation. He who knows this is capable of forgiving everything. Forgiveness is Brahma; forgiveness is Truth […] and it is by forgiveness that the universe is upheld. […] Forgiveness is the strength of the strong; forgiveness is sacrifice; forgiveness is peace of mind.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata III (Vana-parva), section 29 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

> Compassion is the highest virtue. Forgiveness is the highest strength. Self-knowledge is the highest knowledge. There is nothing higher than truth.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata XII (Shanti-parva), section 330 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

The Laws of Manu count forgiveness among the ten marks of duty; their translator of 1833 renders it with a phrase that recalls the Gospel:

> Resignation, the act of returning good for evil, temperance, probity, purity, the restraint of the senses, knowledge of the Sastras, that of the supreme Soul, truthfulness and abstention from anger: such are the ten virtues in which duty consists.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu VI, 92 · after Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

### Judaism

Judaism lays down a demanding rule: God forgives what offends Him, but He does not forgive in the place of the one who has been wronged.

> Man’s faults toward God, the Day of Atonement atones for them; man’s faults toward his neighbor, the Day of Atonement does not atone for them, until he has appeased his neighbor.

<p class="ref">— Mishnah, Yoma 8:9 · working translation from the Hebrew</p>

> What god is your equal [Lord], You who pardon iniquities, who grant grace to offenses […]? You who do not keep Your anger forever, because You delight in benevolence? Yes, You will take us back in pity, You will stifle our iniquities, You will cast all our sins into the depths of the sea.

<p class="ref">— Micah 7:18-19 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

Joseph, once sold by his brothers, refuses to take revenge when they are at his mercy:

> Joseph answered them: Be without fear; for am I in the place of God? You, you had plotted evil against me: God contrived it for good […]. Therefore, be without fear: I will take care of you and your families. And he reassured them and spoke to their hearts.

<p class="ref">— Genesis 50:19-21 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Hatred stirs up quarrels; love covers all faults.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 10:12 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

The opening verses of the Dhammapada describe how resentment works, and the way out of it:

> “I was insulted, I was struck, I was overthrown, I was robbed!” — Those who let themselves go on speaking thus never cease to hate.
>
> “I was insulted, I was struck, I was overthrown, I was robbed!” — Those who do not let themselves go on speaking thus, cease to hate.
>
> “What makes hatreds cease here below is by no means hatred, but rather the absence of hatred.” — That is an axiom as old as the world.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 3-5 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> Let no one deceive another, let him not despise (anyone) in any place, let him not wish ill upon another out of anger or out of resentment.

<p class="ref">— Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipata 147 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

### Christianity

Jesus links the forgiveness we receive from God to the forgiveness we grant: it is the only petition of the Lord’s Prayer that he comments on at once:

> If you forgive men their offenses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you; but if you do not forgive men, your Father will not forgive your offenses either.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6:14-15 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Then Peter came to him, and said: Lord, how many times shall I forgive my brother, when he sins against me? Shall it be up to seven times? Jesus said to him: I do not say to you up to seven times, but up to seventy times seven times.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 18:21-22 · after Segond, 1910</p>

On the cross, Jesus himself intercedes for those who put him to death:

> Jesus said: Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.

<p class="ref">— Luke 23:34 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an makes the same appeal to believers who have been wronged, reminding them that they too hope for God’s forgiveness:

> […] let them rather forgive them *their faults* and pass over them. Would you not *also* wish that God forgive you? He is indulgent and merciful.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 24:22 · after Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

The Qur’an recognizes the right to a retaliation equal to the wrong suffered, but it places forgiveness above it:

> And render for evil an equal evil. He however who forgives and is reconciled *with his adversary,* God shall owe him a reward; for He does not love the oppressors.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 42:40 [Kazimirski XLII, 38] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> It is the wisdom of life to bear with patience and to forgive.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 42:43 [Kazimirski XLII, 41] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

The five traditions see vengeance as an endless cycle and forgiveness as the only way to break it. All of them present forgiveness as **a strength**: “the strength of the strong” (Mahabharata), “the wisdom of life” (Qur’an). Several link the forgiveness one grants to the forgiveness one hopes for: “if you forgive men, your heavenly Father will also forgive you”; “would you not also wish that God forgive you?”

#### Where They Differ

Christianity asks for a forgiveness **without limit** (“seventy times seven times”) and sees its example in Jesus forgiving his executioners. Islam recognizes the right to a reparation equal to the wrong suffered, but places forgiveness above it. Judaism binds forgiveness closely to **repentance**, and lays down a rule stated nowhere else so clearly: God does not forgive in the place of the victim; one must first “appease one’s neighbor.” Buddhism does not speak of a forgiveness granted by God: it describes the letting go of resentment, which frees the offended party first; the evil deed itself bears its fruit under the law of karma.

</div>

## 7. Giving and Care for the Poor

To forgive is to give up what is owed to you; to give is to offer what you do not owe. No religion stops at prayer. All of them ask us to share, and almost all name the same people: the poor, the orphan, the widow, the stranger, the traveler. Several add a strikingly similar demand: to give without ostentation, and without humiliating the one who receives.

### Hinduism

In ancient India, the master who sends his student away first teaches him how to give:

> What one gives, let one give it with faith, not without faith; with joy, with modesty, with awe, with benevolence.

<p class="ref">— Taittiriya Upanishad I, 11, 3 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

The Gita distinguishes the disinterested gift from the calculated gift:

> A gift made with the sense of duty, to a man who cannot repay, a gift made in due time and place and according to merit, is a gift of truth. A present made in the hope of a return or a reward, and as if grudgingly, proceeds from desire.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVII, 20-21 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

An Upanishad relates that the creator god uttered, for the gods, for men and for the demons, a single syllable, “Da,” which each understood according to what he lacked; men heard in it *datta*, “give”:

> The divine voice of the thunder repeats the same thing: Da Da Da, that is to say: Subdue yourselves, Give, Be merciful. Let this triad therefore be taught: self-mastery, giving and mercy.

<p class="ref">— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad V, 2, 3 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

### Judaism

The Torah makes giving a law, and a law that will not lapse as long as there are poor people:

> If there be among you a needy man, from among your brothers, in one of your towns […], you shall not harden your heart, nor shut your hand to your needy brother. Open your hand to him rather! […] Now, there will always be needy people in the land; that is why I give you this charge: open, open your hand to your brother, to the poor, to the needy who shall be in your land!

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 15:7-8 and 11 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

God defines Himself by His care for the most vulnerable:

> who does justice to the orphan and the widow; who shows His love to the stranger, by assuring him bread and clothing. You shall love the stranger, you who were strangers in the land of Egypt!

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 10:18-19 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> To give to the poor is to lend to God, who pays each his due.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 19:17 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud places personal acts of kindness even higher than almsgiving:

> In three respects benevolence is superior to almsgiving: — almsgiving is only the payment of money, but benevolence can also be exercised through personal service. Almsgiving can be given only to the poor, but benevolence can be shown no less toward the rich. Almsgiving is limited to the living, but benevolence can extend to the dead as well as to the living.

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 49b · working translation after Hershon, 1880</p>

### Buddhism

The Buddha counts giving among the simplest virtues, within reach of those who have almost nothing:

> Speak the truth; do not give way to anger; give, to whoever implores you, from the little you have. On these three conditions, you will draw near to the gods.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 224 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> If creatures knew, O monks, the fruit of the distribution of gifts as I know it, they would not eat without having given, and the stain of selfishness would not mark their hearts deeply. Whatever the smallest mouthful, the smallest morsel they possess, if there were someone to receive it, they would not eat without first having shared it.

<p class="ref">— Itivuttaka 26 · working translation after Moore, 1908</p>

### Christianity

At the Last Judgment, according to the Gospel of Matthew, Christ identifies himself with the one who was helped:

> For I was hungry, and you gave me food; I was thirsty, and you gave me drink; I was a stranger, and you took me in; I was naked, and you clothed me; I was sick, and you visited me; I was in prison, and you came to me. […] And the king will answer them: Truly I say to you, whenever you did these things to one of the least of these my brothers, you did them to me.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 25:35-36 and 40 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Pure and undefiled religion, before God our Father, consists in visiting orphans and widows in their affliction, and in keeping oneself unstained by the world.

<p class="ref">— James 1:27 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> But when you give alms, let not your left hand know what your right hand is doing, so that your alms may be given in secret; and your Father, who sees in secret, will reward you.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6:3-4 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The “verse of piety” refuses to reduce religion to ritual:

> Piety does not consist in turning your faces toward the east or the west. Pious is he who believes in God and in the last day, in the angels and in the Book, in the prophets; who, for the love of God, gives of his substance to his kin, to orphans, to the poor, to travelers and to those who ask; who ransoms captives, who observes prayer, who gives alms, fulfills the engagements he contracts, who is patient in adversity […].

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2:177 [Kazimirski II, 172] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> An honest word, the forgiveness of offenses, are worth more than alms followed by pain caused to the one who receives them. God is rich and clement.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2:263 [Kazimirski II, 265] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

A short sura even makes contempt for the orphan and the poor the mark of the one who denies religion:

> What do you think of him who calls this religion a lie?
>
> It is he who repels the orphan,
>
> Who does not urge others to feed the poor.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 107:1-3 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

The five traditions make giving a religious duty, and name almost the same beneficiaries: **the poor, the orphan, the widow, the stranger, the traveler**. They also agree on the **manner** of giving: in secret, without “your left hand knowing what your right hand is doing” (Gospel); without following alms with reproaches (Qur’an); with joy and modesty (Upanishad); without expecting anything in return (Gita); sharing even the last mouthful (Itivuttaka).

#### Where They Differ

The status of the gift differs. In Islam, the legal alms (*zakat*) is an obligatory and quantified pillar, distinct from voluntary alms. In Judaism, the very word for charity, *tzedakah*, means “justice”: to give to the poor is to return to them what is theirs. Christianity sees Christ himself in the poor person who is helped. Buddhism makes giving an exercise in detachment that tears out “the stain of selfishness”; Hinduism judges its worth by the intention, the moment and the person.

</div>

## 8. Honoring Father and Mother

Our first neighbors, finally, are those who gave us life. Respect for parents is probably the duty the world’s religions teach most unanimously. In the Bible as in the Qur’an, it follows immediately after the duties toward God; in the texts of India, parents are treated as divinities or as the worthiest of masters.

### Hinduism

On the day the student leaves his master after studying the Veda, the master gives him his final counsel:

> Do not neglect the works owed to the Gods and to the Fathers! Let your mother be to you as a god! Let your father be to you as a god! Let your teacher be to you as a god! Let your guest be to you as a god!

<p class="ref">— Taittiriya Upanishad I, 11, 2 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

> A teacher is more venerable than ten assistant tutors; a father, than a hundred teachers; a mother is more venerable than a thousand fathers.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu II, 145 · after Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

> By his respect for his mother he obtains this lower world; by his respect for his father, the intermediate world, that of the atmosphere; by his submission to the orders of his spiritual guide, he reaches the heavenly world of Brahma.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu II, 233 · after Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

### Judaism

The Decalogue makes respect for parents the first of the commandments that concern other people:

> Honor your father and your mother, so that your days may be prolonged on the land that the LORD your God will grant you.

<p class="ref">— Exodus 20:12 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Revere, each of you, your mother and your father, and keep my sabbaths: I am the LORD your God. […] Rise before a white head, and honor the person of the old man: fear your God! I am the LORD.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19:3 and 32 · after Zadoc Kahn. Here the mother is named before the father.</p>

The Talmud explains why this commandment touches God himself:

> The rabbis teach that there are three partners in the making of a man: God, the father and the mother.

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Niddah 31a · working translation after Hershon, 1880 (excerpt)</p>

And its model of filial piety is, remarkably, a non-Jew:

> Rav Ulla was once asked: “How far is one bound to honor one’s father and mother?” To which he replied: “See what a Gentile of Ashkelon once did, Dammah ben Nethinah by name. The sages once needed goods worth sixty myriads, which they were ready to pay for at the price asked, but the key to the storehouse happened to be under the pillow of his father, who was fast asleep, and Dammah would not disturb him.”

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Kiddushin 31a · working translation after Hershon, 1880</p>

### Buddhism

The Buddha, who had left his own family to seek Awakening, teaches that the debt owed to parents is never paid off:

> Monks, there are two persons, I declare, who can never be repaid. Which two? Mother and father. Even if one were to carry one’s mother on one shoulder and one’s father on the other, and were to live thus a hundred years […], one could not repay one’s parents. […] Why is that? Monks, parents do much for their children: they raise them, they feed them, they bring them into this world.

<p class="ref">— Anguttara Nikaya II, 4, 2 · working translation after Woodward, 1932</p>

In the teaching he gives to the young layman Sigala, the Buddha describes reciprocal duties:

> In five ways a child should serve his parents […]: — Supported by them once, I shall now be their support; I shall perform the duties that fall to them; I shall maintain the lineage and tradition of my family; I shall make myself worthy of my inheritance. […] In five ways the parents thus served […] show their love for him: they turn him from vice, exhort him to virtue, train him for a profession, arrange a suitable marriage for him and, when the time comes, hand over his inheritance to him.

<p class="ref">— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya III, 189-190 · working translation after T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids, 1921</p>

> To serve one’s mother and father, to protect child and wife, and a peaceful occupation, this is the highest blessing.

<p class="ref">— Mahamangala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 261 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

### Christianity

The apostle Paul takes up the commandment of Moses and stresses its promise:

> Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. Honor your father and your mother (this is the first commandment with a promise), so that you may be happy and live long on the earth. And you, fathers, do not provoke your children, but bring them up with correction and instruction in the Lord.

<p class="ref">— Ephesians 6:1-4 · after Segond, 1910</p>

Jesus reproaches some of his contemporaries for having excused themselves, under the pretext of a religious offering, from helping their parents:

> He answered them: And you, why do you transgress the commandment of God for the sake of your tradition? For God said: Honor your father and your mother; and: Whoever curses his father or his mother shall be put to death.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 15:3-4 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an, like the Bible, places parents right after God:

> God has ordained that you worship none but him, and that you behave well toward your father and mother, whether one of them has reached old age or both have attained it, and they remain with you. Beware of showing them contempt, of reproaching them. Speak to them with respect.
>
> Be humble toward them and full of tenderness, and address this prayer to God: Lord, have mercy on them, just as *they had mercy on me*, as they raised me when I was very small.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 17:23-24 [Kazimirski XVII, 24-25] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

A famous hadith gives the mother a place apart:

> “A man came to the Messenger of God and said: ‘O Messenger of God, who has the greatest right to my good companionship?’ He replied: ‘Your mother.’ — ‘And then?’ — ‘Your mother.’ — ‘And then?’ — ‘Your mother.’ — ‘And then?’ — ‘Then your father.’”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 5971) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Here the traditions are unanimous. In the Bible as in the Qur’an, honoring one’s parents comes **immediately after the duties toward God**; the Upanishad asks that one hold one’s mother and father “as a god.” Several traditions give **the mother** a place apart (“Your mother, your mother, your mother,” says the hadith; “a mother is more venerable than a thousand fathers,” say the Laws of Manu), and several say that we can never repay our parents for what we have received from them.

#### Where They Differ

Some traditions insist on **reciprocity**: Paul asks fathers not to provoke their children, the Buddha enumerates the duties of parents toward their child. The Qur’an sets a limit: one does not obey parents who push one toward idolatry, while continuing to treat them with kindness. Hinduism extends this respect to the spiritual master and to the guest, and prolongs it after death through the worship of ancestors.

</div>

# Part Four — Conduct

<p class="part-intro">After our neighbor, ourselves. Telling the truth, not taking what belongs to others, governing our desires and our anger, guarding against pride: on these everyday virtues the Scriptures agree most plainly.</p>

## 9. Truth and Right Speech

From our duties toward others we turn to the discipline of the self, beginning with speech. Lying, slander and malicious gossip are condemned everywhere. Several traditions go further and treat speech as a spiritual matter in its own right: the tongue can bless or destroy, and whoever masters it masters himself.

### Hinduism

The motto of the Republic of India is taken from an Upanishad:

> Truth triumphs, not falsehood; by truth the path is laid out, the way of the gods, by which the sages of old, their desires fulfilled, advance to where the supreme abode of Truth is found.

<p class="ref">— Mundaka Upanishad III, 1, 6 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

> Speak the truth! Do your duty! Do not neglect the study of the Veda! […] Do not swerve from the truth! Do not swerve from duty!

<p class="ref">— Taittiriya Upanishad I, 11, 1 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

> Measured, truthful speech, full of gentleness, and the practice of pious readings are the austerity of speech.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVII, 15 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Let him speak the truth, let him say things that give pleasure, let him not declare a disagreeable truth, and let him not utter an obliging lie; such is the eternal law.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu IV, 138 · after Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

### Judaism

The Hebrew Bible condemns lying as an offense against God himself:

> The LORD abhors lying lips; but he loves those who act with loyalty.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 12:22 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Do not go about spreading evil among your people, do not be indifferent to the danger of your neighbor: I am the LORD.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19:16 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Keep your tongue from evil, and your lips from treacherous speech; turn away from evil and do good, seek peace and pursue it.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 34:14-15 [Segond 34:13-14] · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud calls slander “the third tongue,” because it claims three victims:

> The third tongue (that is, slander) wounds three parties: the slanderer himself, the one who receives the slander, and the person slandered.

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Arakhin 15b · working translation after Hershon, 1880</p>

### Buddhism

“Right speech” is the third limb of the Noble Eightfold Path, and the Buddha defines it plainly:

> And what, bhikkhus, is right speech? Abstaining from lying, from slander, from harsh words and from idle chatter. This is what is called right speech.

<p class="ref">— Mahasatipatthana Sutta, Digha Nikaya II, 312 · working translation after T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids, 1910</p>

> Better a single word that has meaning than a thousand words devoid of meaning, if it brings calm to the one who hears it.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 100 · after Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

Jesus asks for speech so upright that it no longer needs an oath:

> Let your word be yes, yes, no, no; whatever is added to this comes from the evil one.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5:37 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Therefore, renounce falsehood, and let each of you speak the truth to his neighbor; for we are members of one another.

<p class="ref">— Ephesians 4:25 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Likewise, the tongue is a small member, and it boasts of great things. Behold, how a small fire can set a great forest ablaze! The tongue also is a fire; it is the world of iniquity. […] With it we bless the Lord our Father, and with it we curse men made in the image of God. From the same mouth come blessing and cursing. My brothers, this ought not to be so.

<p class="ref">— James 3:5-6 and 9-10 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an makes upright speech a commandment addressed to all believers:

> O believers! fear the Lord; speak with uprightness.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 33:70 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Do not clothe the truth in the robe of falsehood; Do not hide the truth when you know it.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2:42 [Kazimirski II, 39] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

The Qur’an compares backbiting to an act everyone finds monstrous:

> O you who believe! avoid too frequent suspicion; there are suspicions that are sins; do not seek to spy on the steps of others, do not speak ill of one another; which of you would wish to eat the flesh of his dead brother? — Does it disgust you? — Then fear God.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 49:12 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> “Truthfulness leads to piety, and piety leads to Paradise; a man keeps speaking the truth until he becomes a truthful one. Lying leads to perversity, and perversity leads to the Fire; a man keeps lying until he is recorded with God as a liar.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 6094) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

All the traditions condemn lying, false witness and malicious gossip, and all see in speech a **formidable power**: the tongue “is a fire” (James), gossip amounts to “eating the flesh of one’s dead brother” (Qur’an), “right speech” is a limb of the Buddhist Noble Path. All also make truth a **path**: “truth triumphs” (Upanishad), “truthfulness leads to piety” (hadith).

#### Where They Differ

The texts do not set the limit in the same place. The Laws of Manu ask us not to “declare a disagreeable truth”: here truth gives way to kindness. Jesus asks his followers to give up oaths, because a plain word should be enough. Judaism forbids speaking ill of another **even when it is true**, and the Talmud counts three victims of slander. Buddhism also counts **idle chatter** among the faults of speech.

</div>

## 10. Honesty and Justice

After words, deeds: the truth we owe in our speech we also owe in our dealings. “You shall not steal”: the prohibition appears in the Decalogue, among the five precepts of Buddhism, in the lists of duties of Hinduism. But the texts go beyond armed robbery. They target the merchant’s rigged scales, the short measure, the deposit never returned: ordinary dishonesty, the kind anyone can commit without thinking of himself as a thief.

### Hinduism

Beyond the duties proper to each class of society, the Laws of Manu set out a duty common to all:

> To refrain from doing harm, to always speak the truth, to abstain from all theft, to be pure, and to restrain one’s organs, this is in sum the duty prescribed by Manu to the four classes.

<p class="ref">— Laws of Manu X, 63 · after Loiseleur-Deslongchamps, 1833</p>

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali make these same prohibitions the first step of the spiritual life:

> Abstention from harming, abstention from lying, from theft, from incontinence and from the acceptance of gifts: such are the abstentions.

<p class="ref">— Yoga Sutras of Patanjali II, 30 · working translation after Woods, 1914</p>

### Judaism

Leviticus lists the forms of theft with precision, down to the most discreet:

> You shall not commit theft, nor denial nor fraud to the detriment of your neighbor.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19:11 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Do not commit iniquity in matters of judgment, of weights and of measures. Have exact scales, exact weights, an exact ephah, an exact hin: I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt.

<p class="ref">— Leviticus 19:35-36 · after Zadoc Kahn. The ephah and the hin (*hîn*) are measures of grain and of liquids.</p>

According to the Talmud, the first question put to a man before the heavenly court concerns neither his prayers nor his study:

> At the hour when a man is brought to judgment, he is asked: Did you conduct your business with integrity? Did you set fixed times for the Torah? Did you occupy yourself with founding a family? Did you hope for deliverance? […]

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a (saying of Rava) · working translation from the Aramaic</p>

### Buddhism

The second Buddhist precept forbids taking what has not been given, and even approving of it in others:

> Let the disciple abstain from taking, in any place whatsoever, anything whatsoever that has not been given to him, knowing (that it belongs to another); let him not cause anyone to take, let him not approve those who take; let him avoid every (kind of) theft.

<p class="ref">— Dhammika Sutta, Sutta Nipata 394 · working translation after Fausböll, 1881</p>

The Dhammapada gathers in two stanzas the breaches of the five precepts, and states their consequence:

> He who destroys a life, who speaks lying words, who takes in this world what is not given to him, who approaches another’s wife,
>
> And who gives himself over to strong drink, — that man, in this world, tears up his own roots himself.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 246-247 · after Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

Paul turns the prohibition into a duty: the repentant thief must work so as to give in turn:

> Let him who stole steal no more; but rather let him labor, doing with his hands what is good, so that he may have something to give to him who is in need.

<p class="ref">— Ephesians 4:28 · after Segond, 1910</p>

John the Baptist, who prepares the coming of Jesus, gives each person a rule of justice fitted to his trade:

> Tax collectors also came to be baptized, and they said to him: Master, what must we do? He answered them: Exact nothing beyond what has been ordered you. Soldiers also asked him: And we, what must we do? He answered them: Commit neither extortion nor fraud against anyone, and be content with your pay.

<p class="ref">— Luke 3:12-14 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Indeed, the commandments: You shall not commit adultery, you shall not kill, you shall not steal, you shall not covet, and whatever others there may be, are summed up in this word: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. Love does no harm to the neighbor: love is therefore the fulfillment of the law.

<p class="ref">— Romans 13:9-10 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an, born in a city of merchants, curses the rigged scales:

> Woe to those who falsify the measure or the weight,
>
> Who, when buying, demand a full measure,
>
> And who, when they measure or weigh for others, cheat them!

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 83:1-3 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> When you measure, fill the measure. Weigh with a just balance. This is better, and fairer in the final result.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 17:35 [Kazimirski XVII, 37] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> God commands you to return the deposit to whom it belongs, and to judge your fellows with equity. It is a fine deed that God recommends to you. He hears and sees all.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 4:58 [Kazimirski IV, 61] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

The prohibition of theft appears in all the fundamental lists: the Decalogue, the five Buddhist precepts, the abstentions of yoga, the “duty common to the four classes” of the Laws of Manu. All the traditions also target **ordinary fraud** (the short measure, the rigged scales, the deposit never returned), and the Qur’an, like the Torah, makes honest measure a religious matter.

#### Where They Differ

The emphases differ. Buddhism forbids taking, causing others to take, and approving of theft; yoga goes as far as refusing to **accept gifts**; Paul turns the ban into a positive duty, working “so as to have something to give,” and roots it in love of neighbor.

</div>

## 11. Self-Mastery: Fasting, Desires, Anger

No one who is master of himself steals or lies: the Scriptures trace vice back to its root, unbridled desire. All the traditions regard a person ruled by appetite and anger as someone who is not free. All of them offer a discipline for taking back the reins: fasting, temperance, vigilance. And several say, in almost the same words, that victory over oneself is the greatest victory of all.

### Hinduism

The Gita traces, with remarkable precision, the chain that leads from a mere glance to inner ruin:

> In the man who contemplates the objects of the senses, an inclination toward them is born; from this inclination is born desire; from desire, violent appetite; From this appetite, disturbance of thought; from this disturbance, the wandering of memory; from the ruin of memory, the loss of reason; and by this loss, he is lost.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 62-63 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Hell has three gates through which they are lost: lust, anger and greed. They must therefore be avoided.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVI, 21 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

But it rejects the excesses of asceticism as much as those of appetite:

> The divine Union is neither for him who eats too much, nor for him who eats nothing; it is neither for him who sleeps long, nor for him who is always awake, Arjuna. The holy Union, which takes away all ills, is for him who eats in measure, recreates himself in measure, acts, sleeps and wakes in measure.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita VI, 16-17 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

The only fast prescribed by the Torah is that of the Day of Atonement. But the prophet read in the synagogue that day reminds his hearers what God expects of a fast:

> Is this a fast that can be pleasing to me, a day when a man mortifies himself? […] But this is the fast that I love: to break the chains of injustice, to untie the bonds of every yoke, to send free those who are oppressed, to shatter at last every servitude; then further, to share your bread with the hungry, to take into your house the wretched without shelter; when you see a naked man, to cover him […]!

<p class="ref">— Isaiah 58:5-7 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Rend your hearts and not your garments, and return to the LORD, your God, for he is clement, merciful, slow to anger and abounding in grace.

<p class="ref">— Joel 2:13 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> He who resists anger prevails over the hero; he who governs his passions, over a taker of cities.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 16:32 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

The Dhammapada says it with the image of a warrior:

> One might, in a single encounter, vanquish thousands upon thousands of men: to vanquish oneself alone is the most glorious of victories.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 103 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> Let one rid oneself of anger! Let one rid oneself of pride! Let one shake off all one’s bonds! […]
>
> He whose anger has been given free rein, but who holds it back like a chariot in motion, — that man I call a charioteer. The rest of men merely hold the reins.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 221-222 · after Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

Jesus does not abolish fasting; he asks that it remain a secret between the believer and God:

> When you fast, do not put on a sad look, like the hypocrites, who disfigure their faces to show men that they are fasting. […] But when you fast, anoint your head and wash your face, so as not to show men that you are fasting, but your Father who is there in the secret place; and your Father, who sees in secret, will reward you.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6:16-18 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control; the law is not against these things.

<p class="ref">— Galatians 5:22-23 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> So let every man be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not accomplish the justice of God.

<p class="ref">— James 1:19-20 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an institutes the fast of the month of Ramadan and ties it to the religions that came before:

> O believers! fasting is prescribed for you, just as it was prescribed for those who came before you. Fear the Lord.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2:183 [Kazimirski II, 179] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> To those who give alms in ease as in hardship, who know how to master their anger, and who forgive the men *who offend them*. Truly God loves those who act with kindness.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 3:134 [Kazimirski III, 128] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> “The strong man is not he who throws others down in wrestling; the strong man is only he who masters himself when he is angry.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 6114) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

For all the traditions, a person ruled by appetite and anger is not free, and several say in the same terms that **victory over oneself is the greatest**: “to vanquish oneself alone is the most glorious of victories” (Dhammapada), “the strong man is he who masters himself when he is angry” (hadith), “he who resists anger prevails over the hero” (Proverbs). All know **fasting**, and the Qur’an expressly says it was “prescribed for those who came before you.” Hinduism and Buddhism recommend the same **middle way**, between indulgence and mortification.

#### Where They Differ

Fasting is a fixed obligation in Islam (the month of Ramadan) and in Judaism (the day of Yom Kippur in particular); in the Gospel it is a freer, discreet act of piety; the Gita condemns austerity that tortures the body. For Paul, self-mastery is a “fruit of the Spirit,” a gift received more than a conquest. Buddhism and Islam also forbid intoxicating drinks.

</div>
## 12. Humility

Self-mastery has one last adversary, subtler than hunger or anger. In all five traditions, pride is a major obstacle: it shuts a person off from God, from others and from the truth about himself. Humility, in these texts, means seeing clearly, like the man who notices the beam in his own eye before looking for the speck in his neighbor’s.

### Hinduism

Among the qualities that make up true knowledge, the Gita names first the absence of pride, which Burnouf renders as “modesty”:

> Modesty, sincerity, gentleness, patience, uprightness, respect for the teacher, purity, constancy, mastery over oneself;

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XIII, 7 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

> Hypocrisy, pride, vanity, anger, harshness of speech, ignorance — such, son of Pritha, are the marks of him who is born into the condition of the Asuras.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVI, 4 · after Burnouf, 1861. The Asuras are the demonic powers.</p>

> Let a man, having cast them all out, walk without desires, without greed, without pride; he walks toward peace.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 71 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

A prophet sums up the whole of religion in a single sentence, and humility is part of it:

> Man, you have been told what is good, what the Lord asks of you: nothing but to practice justice, to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God!

<p class="ref">— Micah 6:8 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Pride goes before ruin; arrogance is the forerunner of the fall.

<p class="ref">— Proverbs 16:18 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud explains why, in the controversies, the law followed the school of Hillel rather than that of Shammai:

> His disciples were gentle and patient, for while upholding their own rulings, they also set forth those maintained by the school of Shammai, and often even mentioned the positions of the school of Shammai first […]. This teaches us that whoever humbles himself, God will raise up; and whoever raises himself up, God will bring low. Whoever pursues greatness, greatness will flee from him; and whoever flees from greatness, greatness will pursue him.

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 13b · working translation after Hershon, 1880 (excerpt)</p>

### Buddhism

The Buddha teaches humility through irony:

> The fool who knows that he is a fool is, in that at least, a wise man. But the fool who believes himself wise — of him it is said: “he is a fool.”

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 63 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> It is not on the transgressions of others, on the deeds or omissions of others, that he should fix his attention, but on what he himself has done or left undone.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 50 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> Easy to see is the fault of another, hard to see one’s own. The faults of others one brings out as much as one can; one’s own, by contrast, one hides as the cheat hides the kali from his partner.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 252 · after Hû, 1878. The “kali” is the losing throw in the game of dice.</p>

### Christianity

The Beatitudes open with the “poor in spirit”:

> Blessed are the poor in spirit, for the kingdom of heaven is theirs!

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5:3 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Why do you see the speck that is in your brother’s eye, but don’t consider the beam that is in your own eye?

<p class="ref">— Matthew 7:3 · World English Bible</p>

> For whoever exalts himself will be brought low, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.

<p class="ref">— Luke 14:11 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Do nothing out of a spirit of faction or out of vainglory, but let humility make you regard others as being above yourselves.

<p class="ref">— Philippians 2:3 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an recognizes the servants of God by the way they walk:

> The servants of the Merciful are those who walk with modesty upon this earth, and who say: PEACE BE WITH YOU, to the ignorant who address them.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 25:63 [Kazimirski XXV, 64] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

The sage Luqman, a figure of proverbial wisdom, counsels his son thus:

> Do not twist your mouth *in disdain* for men; let your walk not be proud, for God does not love the presumptuous, boastful man.
>
> Seek to moderate your step and to lower your voice, for the most unpleasant of voices is indeed the voice of the donkey.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 31:18-19 [Kazimirski XXXI, 17-18] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> Do not walk pompously upon the earth; you could neither split it in two nor equal the height of the mountains.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 17:37 [Kazimirski XVII, 39] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Pride is the enemy in every tradition. All of them ask us to **walk with modesty** upon the earth and to see our own faults before those of others (here the Dhammapada meets the Gospel image of the speck and the beam), and all promise exaltation to the one who humbles himself.

#### Where They Differ

Christian humility has a unique model: the self-abasement of God himself in Jesus, who “emptied himself” (Philippians 2:7). In Hinduism, pride is *ahankara*, the sense of being a separate “I,” which the sage abandons. In Buddhism, it is one of the fetters that bind one to the cycle of rebirths, and its root is the illusion of a permanent self.

</div>

# Part Five — The Inner Life

<p class="part-intro">Deeds are not enough. All the traditions also ask us to turn inward: to pray or to meditate, to purify the intention from which deeds are born, and to loosen the grip of goods that pass away.</p>

## 13. Prayer and Meditation

Humility opens the door to the inner life, and the first of its practices is prayer. In the religions born of Abraham, believers pray to God; in Buddhism, they meditate; in Hinduism, they do both. The gestures and words differ, and the difference is real: prayer is addressed to someone, the meditation described in the early Buddhist canon to no one — the Great Vehicle, for its part, also has prayers addressed to buddhas. Yet all these practices share a striking aim, to gather a scattered mind, and several texts ask that they be done in secret, away from the eyes of others.

### Hinduism

The most sacred of the Vedic formulas, the Gayatri, has been recited every day by millions of Hindus for three thousand years:

> We adore the noble light of the divine Savitri, who himself calls forth our prayers.

<p class="ref">— Rig-Veda III, 62, 10 · after Langlois, 1848-1851</p>

> Lead me from the unreal to the real! Lead me from darkness to light! Lead me from death to immortality!

<p class="ref">— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad I, 3, 28 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

The Gita also teaches silent meditation, apart, with the mind gathered:

> Let the Yogi always practice his devotion alone, apart, without company, master of his thought, stripped of hopes. […] The Yogi is like a lamp which, sheltered from the wind, does not flicker when, having subdued his thought, he gives himself to the mystical Union.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita VI, 10 and 19 · after Burnouf, 1861. “Union” is Burnouf’s constant rendering of the word *yoga*.</p>

### Judaism

The Shema asks that the word of God accompany every moment of the day:

> These duties that I lay upon you today shall be engraved on your heart. You shall instill them in your children and speak of them, whether in your house or on the road, when you lie down and when you rise.

<p class="ref">— Deuteronomy 6:6-7 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> The LORD is near to all who call upon him, to all who call upon him in sincerity.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 145:18 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> I keep my eyes constantly fixed on the Lord; if he is at my right hand, I shall not falter.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 16:8 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

The sages warn against prayer that has become routine:

> Rabbi Simeon said: Be attentive in the reading of the Shema and in prayer. And when you pray, do not make your prayer a fixed thing, but a supplication and an entreaty before God, blessed be He […].

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot 2:13 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>

### Buddhism

Buddhism does not pray to a God, but it asks for vigilance at every moment:

> Vigilance is the path that leads to deliverance from death; negligence is the one that leads to death. The vigilant do not die; the negligent are already as if dead.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 21 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> On his wavering, restless thought, hard to contain, hard to master, the intelligent man imposes the same straightness as a fletcher on an arrow.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 33 · after Hû, 1878</p>

The discourse on mindfulness presents meditation as the way of purification:

> The one way, bhikkhus, that leads to the purification of beings, to the overcoming of sorrow and lamentation, to the extinction of pain and distress, to the acquisition of the right method, to the realization of Nirvana, is that of the four foundations of mindfulness.

<p class="ref">— Mahasatipaṭṭhana Sutta, Digha Nikaya II, 290 · working translation after T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids, 1910</p>

### Christianity

Jesus teaches prayer in secret, then gives his disciples the very words of their prayer:

> When you pray, do not be like the hypocrites, who love to pray standing in the synagogues and at the street corners, to be seen by men. Truly I say to you, they receive their reward. But when you pray, go into your room, shut your door, and pray to your Father who is there in the secret place; and your Father, who sees in secret, will repay you.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6:5-6 · after Segond, 1910</p>

The prayer taught by Jesus, common to all the Churches:

> Our Father who art in heaven! Hallowed be thy name; thy kingdom come; thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread; forgive us our offenses, as we also forgive those who have offended us; lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6:9-13 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Be always joyful. Pray without ceasing. Give thanks in all things, for this is the will of God toward you in Jesus Christ.

<p class="ref">— 1 Thessalonians 5:16-18 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The *Fatiha*, “the Opening,” is recited in every ritual prayer, that is, at least seventeen times a day:

> Praise be to God, master of the universe,
>
> The clement, the merciful,
>
> Sovereign on the day of retribution.
>
> It is you we adore, it is you whose help we implore.
>
> Guide us on the straight path,
>
> On the path of those whom you have showered with your blessings,
>
> Not of those who have incurred your wrath, nor of those who go astray.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 1:2-7 [Kazimirski I, 1-7] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> When my servants speak to you of me, I shall be near them; I shall answer the prayer of the suppliant who implores me […].

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2:186 [Kazimirski II, 182] · after Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

> […] What! is it not in the remembrance of God that the hearts of men find rest!

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 13:28 · after Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt). The “remembrance” is the *dhikr*, the recollection of God.</p>

> […] perform the prayer, for prayer preserves from turpitudes, from blameworthy deeds […].

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 29:45 [Kazimirski XXIX, 44] · after Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

All the traditions ask us to **gather the mind** and to turn toward what lies beyond it, often at regular hours: the *Fatiha* is recited at every Muslim prayer, the Gayatri every day for three thousand years, Jewish prayer marks the rhythm of morning and evening. Several texts recommend **secrecy** and solitude (“go into your room, shut your door,” says the Gospel; “alone, apart,” says the Gita), and all expect prayer to **transform conduct**: “prayer preserves from turpitudes” (Qur’an).

#### Where They Differ

Prayer is addressed to **someone**; the meditation of the early Buddhist canon, to no one. Hinduism knows both, the hymn to the god and silent meditation on the Self. The forms vary too: five ritual prayers at fixed hours in Islam, three daily services in Judaism, “pray without ceasing” in Paul, the foundation of mindfulness in Buddhism.

</div>

## 14. The Heart and the Intention

To pray or to meditate is to turn inward, and the Scriptures place the worth of our deeds there too. The five traditions refuse to judge people by the outward show of their deeds and rites: what counts, they say, is the heart, the mind, the intention behind them. A religious act performed without inner purity is worth nothing; the humblest act offered from an upright heart is worth a great deal.

### Hinduism

For the God of the Gita, an offering is worth the devotion that goes with it, whatever its price:

> When a leaf, a flower, a fruit, or water is offered to me in adoration, I receive them as food, as a pious offering. So then, what you do, what you eat, what you sacrifice, what you give, what you inflict upon yourself, O son of Kunti, make of it an offering to me.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita IX, 26-27 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

> But every sacrifice, every gift, every penance, every deed performed without Faith is called evil, son of Pritha, and is nothing, neither in this life nor in the other.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita XVII, 28 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

> He who, having cast out desire, performs works with God in view, is no more stained by sin than the lotus leaf is by water.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita V, 10 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

When Samuel seeks among the sons of Jesse the one whom God has chosen as king, he first lets himself be impressed by appearance:

> But God said to Samuel: “Do not consider his looks or his tall stature; that one I reject. What man sees does not count: man sees only the outside; God looks at the heart.”

<p class="ref">— 1 Samuel 16:7 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> O God, create in me a pure heart, and cause an upright spirit to be born anew within my breast. […] the sacrifices [pleasing] to God are a contrite spirit; a broken and downcast heart, O God, you do not disdain.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 51:12 and 19 [Segond 51:10 and 17] · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

The Talmud draws from it a brief maxim:

> The Holy One, blessed be He, wants the heart, as it is written: “And the LORD looks at the heart.”

<p class="ref">— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 106b · working translation from the Aramaic</p>

> Who shall ascend the mountain of the Lord? Who shall stand in his holy dwelling? He whose hands are without stain, whose heart is pure […].

<p class="ref">— Psalm 24:3-4 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

### Buddhism

The Dhammapada opens with this statement, which the tradition holds to be the foundation of the whole teaching:

> In the proper nature of beings, the inner sense holds the first place; the inner sense is what is most eminent; the inner sense makes them what they are. Whoever speaks or acts with a corrupted inner sense — him pain follows, as the wheel follows the foot of the animal that draws (the cart).

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 1 · after Hû, 1878. The “inner sense” is the mind (*manas*).</p>

> To abstain from all evil, to do good, to purify one’s thought — such are the commandments of the Buddhas.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 183 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> What use are these braided locks? What use a skirt of goatskin? Within you is utter chaos; you tend only the outside.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 394 · after Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

Jesus promises the sight of God to the pure in heart, and places in the heart the source of good and evil alike:

> Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God!

<p class="ref">— Matthew 5:8 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> But what comes out of the mouth proceeds from the heart, and that is what defiles a man. For out of the heart come evil thoughts, murders, adulteries, fornications, thefts, false witness, slanders. These are the things that defile a man; but to eat without having washed one’s hands does not defile a man.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 15:18-20 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> The good man brings good things out of his good treasure, and the evil man brings evil things out of his evil treasure.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 12:35 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The first hadith of al-Bukhari’s collection, and of an-Nawawi’s *Forty Hadiths*, lays down the principle:

> “Deeds are worth only by their intentions, and each man has only what he intended.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by al-Bukhari (no. 1) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

> “God looks neither at your appearances nor at your possessions, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds.”

<p class="ref">— Hadith reported by Muslim (no. 2564) · working translation from the Arabic</p>

Concerning the sacrifice of the pilgrimage, the Qur’an says what reaches God:

> The flesh and the blood of the victims do not reach God; but your piety rises up to him […].

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 22:37 [Kazimirski XXII, 38] · after Kazimirski, 1869 (excerpt)</p>

> On the day when riches and children will be of no use,
>
> Except to him who comes to God with a sound heart.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 26:88-89 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

This may be the deepest convergence of all. The five traditions judge the deed **by the intention that carries it**: “deeds are worth only by their intentions” (hadith), “the inner sense makes beings what they are” (Dhammapada), “out of the heart come evil thoughts” (Gospel), and the God of the Gita receives a simple leaf offered with devotion. All denounce **outward religiosity**: the flesh of the victims “does not reach God” (Qur’an); “you tend only the outside” (Dhammapada); the Hebrew Bible recalls that God looks at the heart.

#### Where They Differ

Judaism and Islam maintain a precise ritual law, while demanding the intention that animates it (*kavana*, *niyya*). Christianity has relativized the rules of ritual purity in favor of purity of heart alone. Hinduism also knows a ritual purity distinct from inner purity. Buddhism places intention at the center of the moral act: intention is what produces karma.

</div>

## 15. Detachment and Impermanence

A pure heart is also a heart free of what passes away. And everything passes: wealth, health, life itself. The five traditions return to this again and again, without morbidity. They do not ask us to despise the world, only to stay free of its chains and to place our “treasure” where no one can steal or destroy it.

### Hinduism

The heart of the Gita’s teaching is action without attachment to its fruits:

> Be attentive to the performance of works, never to their fruits; do not do the work for the fruit it yields, but do not seek to avoid the work. Steadfast in the mystical Union, perform the work and cast out desire; be equal in success and in reverses; Union is equanimity of soul.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 47-48 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

> The meetings of the elements that cause cold and heat, pleasure and pain, come and go and are not eternal. Bear them, son of Kunti.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 14 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

> All this, all that moves upon the earth, must be enveloped in the Lord (the Self). When you have renounced all that, then you may enjoy. Do not covet anyone’s goods!

<p class="ref">— Isha Upanishad 1 · working translation after Müller, 1879</p>

### Judaism

The only psalm that tradition attributes to Moses meditates on the brevity of life:

> The span of our life is seventy years, and, at the most, eighty years; and all their splendor is but toil and misery. For quickly the thread is cut, and we fly away. […] Teach us, then, to count our days, that we may acquire a heart open to wisdom.

<p class="ref">— Psalm 90:10 and 12 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

Job, stripped in a single day of all his goods, answers with these words, which the Jewish funeral liturgy takes up:

> Naked I came out of my mother’s womb, and naked I shall return there. The LORD had given, the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD!

<p class="ref">— Job 1:21 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> He who loves money is never satisfied with money; he who loves opulence gets no profit from it: this too is vanity!

<p class="ref">— Ecclesiastes 5:9 [Segond 5:10] · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Ben Zoma said: Who is wise? He who learns from every man […]. Who is strong? He who masters his inclination […]. Who is rich? He who rejoices in his portion […].

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot 4:1 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>

### Buddhism

Impermanence is the first of the three marks of existence, and to understand it fully is already a deliverance:

> “All aggregations are transient.” When one is thoroughly penetrated by this fact, one is delivered from pain. This is the way of purification.
>
> “All aggregations are subject to pain.” When one is thoroughly penetrated by this fact, one is delivered from pain. This is the way of purification.
>
> “All forms are without substantial reality.” When one is thoroughly penetrated by this fact, one is delivered from pain. This is the way of purification.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 277-279 · after Hû, 1878. The “aggregations” are compounded things.</p>

These were also the last words of the Buddha:

> Then the Blessed One addressed the brethren and said: “Behold, brethren, I exhort you: decay is inherent in all compounded things! Work out your salvation with diligence!” This was the last word of the Tathagata.

<p class="ref">— Mahaparinibbana Sutta VI, 10 · working translation after Rhys Davids, 1881</p>

### Christianity

Jesus contrasts the treasures of earth with those of heaven:

> Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy, and where thieves break in and steal; but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where moth and rust do not destroy, and where thieves neither break in nor steal. For where your treasure is, there also will your heart be.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 6:19-21 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Guard yourselves carefully against all covetousness; for a man’s life does not depend on his possessions, even were he in abundance. […] But God said to him: Fool! this very night your soul will be required of you; and what you have prepared, whose will it be?

<p class="ref">— Luke 12:15 and 20 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> for we brought nothing into the world, and it is evident that we can carry nothing out of it; if then we have food and clothing, that will suffice us.

<p class="ref">— 1 Timothy 6:7-8 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> You who do not know what will happen tomorrow! for what is your life? You are a vapor that appears for a little while, and then vanishes.

<p class="ref">— James 4:14 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an compares the life of this world to grass that the rain makes grow and the wind scatters:

> Set before them the parable of the life of this world. It is like the water that we send down from the sky; the plants of the earth mingle with it; the next day they are dry; the winds scatter them. For God is all-powerful.
>
> Riches and children are the ornaments of the life of this world; but the things that remain, good works, will yield more with your Lord as reward and as hope.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 18:45-46 [Kazimirski XVIII, 43-44] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> The desire to increase your riches preoccupies you
>
> Until the moment you go down into the grave;
>
> […]
>
> Then you will be questioned about the pleasures of this world.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 102:1-2 and 8 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> All that is on the earth will pass away.
>
> The face of God alone will remain, surrounded with majesty and glory.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 55:26-27 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Everything passes: the five traditions repeat it. “All aggregations are transient” (Dhammapada); “All that is on the earth will pass away” (Qur’an); “You are a vapor” (James); pleasure and pain “are not eternal” (Gita). And all draw the same lesson from it: do not be chained to riches, and place your **treasure** where nothing can destroy it, “in heaven,” says the Gospel, in “the things that remain, good works,” says the Qur’an.

#### Where They Differ

Detachment does not mean fleeing the world. The Gita demands **action**, renouncing only its fruit. Judaism and Islam hold wealth honestly acquired and justly shared to be a blessing. The Gospel goes further in its warning against money. Buddhism alone draws a further conclusion from impermanence: there is no permanent self to cling to either.

</div>

# Part Six — Destiny

<p class="part-intro">What becomes of our deeds, and what becomes of us? On the law that binds the deed to its fruit, the five voices speak almost in unison. On what awaits us beyond death, they begin to part ways. And in each tradition, mystics claim that something of that beyond can be tasted here and now.</p>

## 16. Acts and Their Fruits

If everything passes, our deeds remain: nothing we do is lost. This conviction runs through all five traditions: good and evil bear fruit, and no one escapes what he has sown. The farming image returns from text to text. But who ripens the harvest? A God who judges, for the religions born of Abraham; an impersonal law, karma, for those of India.

### Hinduism

One of the earliest statements of the law of karma appears in the Upanishads:

> As one acts and as one conducts oneself, so one becomes: the man of good deeds becomes good, the man of evil deeds becomes evil. One becomes pure by pure acts, evil by evil acts. And it is said further: man is made of desires. As is his desire, so is his will; as is his will, so is his act; and whatever act he performs, he will reap its fruit.

<p class="ref">— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad IV, 4, 5 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

> He who acts with hostility toward another (in this life) becomes the victim of similar acts performed by that other. Whatever acts one performs, in whatever body it may be, one must suffer their consequences in those bodies.

<p class="ref">— Mahabharata XIII (Anuśasana-parva), section 116 · working translation after Ganguli, 1883-1896</p>

And the good accomplished is never lost, even for one who has not attained perfection:

> Son of Pritha, neither here below nor beyond can that man be destroyed: a man of good, my friend, never enters the unhappy way.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita VI, 40 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

### Judaism

The prophets of Israel recall that God sees into the depths of the heart, and repays each according to his deeds:

> I, the LORD, I search the hearts, I probe the reins; I know how to repay each one according to his ways, according to the fruit of his works.

<p class="ref">— Jeremiah 17:10 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> For they sow the wind and they reap the whirlwind.

<p class="ref">— Hosea 8:7 · after Zadoc Kahn (excerpt)</p>

Against a proverb that made the children pay for the faults of the fathers, the prophet Ezekiel affirms that each person answers for himself:

> It is the person who sins who shall die: the son shall not bear the fault of the father, nor the father the fault of the son; the righteousness of the righteous is credited to the righteous, the wickedness of the wicked to the wicked.

<p class="ref">— Ezekiel 18:20 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

> Consider three things, and you will not fall into the hands of transgression: know what is above you — an eye that sees, an ear that hears, and all your deeds written in a book.

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot 2:1 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>

### Buddhism

The Dhammapada affirms that no place in the world lets us escape our deeds:

> There is no place in this world, nor in the air, nor in the midst of the ocean, nor in the depths of the mountains, where one may be rid of the evil one has done.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 127 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> One will suffer oneself from an evil deed one has done. By not doing it, one purifies oneself. Pure or impure, each one is so by himself; no one purifies another.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 165 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> Here below, as after his death, in both cases, the good man rejoices. “I have done good,” he says, rejoicing. Greater still is his joy as he advances along the good path.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 18 · after Hû, 1878</p>

### Christianity

Paul takes up the image of sowing and harvest:

> Be not deceived: God is not mocked. What a man has sown, that he will also reap. He who sows for his flesh will reap corruption from the flesh; but he who sows for the Spirit will reap eternal life from the Spirit.

<p class="ref">— Galatians 6:7-8 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Judge not, that you be not judged. For you will be judged with the judgment with which you judge, and you will be measured with the measure with which you measure.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 7:1-2 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each may receive according to the good or the evil he has done while in his body.

<p class="ref">— 2 Corinthians 5:10 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an weighs deeds down to the weight of an atom:

> He who has done good of the weight of an atom shall see it,
>
> And he who has committed evil of the weight of an atom shall see it also.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 99:7-8 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> The soul that bears its own burden shall not bear that of another.
>
> Man shall have only what he has earned.
>
> His efforts shall be appraised *at their true worth*,
>
> He shall be rewarded for them with a scrupulous recompense.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 53:38-41 [Kazimirski LIII, 39-42] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

No deed is lost. “What a man has sown, that he will also reap” (Paul); “whatever deed he performs, he will gather its fruit” (Upanishad); good and evil “of the weight of an atom” shall be seen (Qur’an); there is no place in the world where one may “be rid of the evil one has done” (Dhammapada). All of them also insist on **personal responsibility**: no one bears the burden of another (Qur’an), no one purifies another (Dhammapada).

#### Where They Differ

Who ripens the harvest, and when? For Judaism, Christianity and Islam, **God judges**, at the end of a single life. For Hinduism and Buddhism, an **impersonal law**, karma, works from one life to the next. Christianity holds this judgment together with **grace**, which saves beyond all merit; Islam, with the mercy of God; the Gita, with the devotion that frees man from the bonds of his deeds.

</div>

## 17. Death and What Follows It

There remains the final reckoning, when deeds bear their fruit. No tradition looks away from death. All of them ask us to prepare for it, and all affirm that death does not have the last word. They hope for different things beyond it: resurrection and life with God for some, deliverance from the cycle of rebirths for others. Here the agreement of the Scriptures reaches its limit; the next chapter listens to those who sought to taste that end in this life.

### Hinduism

On the battlefield, Krishna consoles Arjuna, who dreads killing his own kin:

> As one casts off worn-out garments to take new ones, so the Soul casts off worn-out bodies to put on new bodies.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita II, 22 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

Having come to question Yama, the god of death, about what remains after it, the young Naciketas receives this answer:

> The knowing Self is not born, it does not die; it sprang from nothing, and nothing sprang from it. The Ancient One is unborn, eternal, everlasting; he is not killed when the body is killed.

<p class="ref">— Katha Upanishad I, 2, 18 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

The ultimate goal, however, is deliverance from the cycle of rebirth:

> As the flowing rivers disappear into the sea, losing their name and their form, so the sage, freed from name and form, goes to the divine Person, greater than the great. He who knows this supreme Brahman becomes Brahman. […] He overcomes sorrow, he overcomes evil; freed from the fetters of the heart, he becomes immortal.

<p class="ref">— Mundaka Upanishad III, 2, 8-9 · working translation after Müller, 1884</p>

### Judaism

The Hebrew Bible speaks of death with great restraint:

> let the dust return to the dust, becoming again what it was, and let the spirit go back to God who gave it.

<p class="ref">— Ecclesiastes 12:7 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

Yet one of its latest books affirms the resurrection plainly:

> Many of those who sleep in the dust of the ground shall awake, some to everlasting life, others to be an object of shame and everlasting horror.

<p class="ref">— Daniel 12:2 · after Zadoc Kahn</p>

The sages of the Mishnah invite us to live this life as a preparation:

> Rabbi Jacob said: This world is like a vestibule before the world to come; prepare yourself in the vestibule, so that you may enter the hall.

<p class="ref">— Pirkei Avot 4:16 · working translation after Taylor, 1897</p>

### Buddhism

The Dhammapada recalls that no one can protect us from death:

> Children, a father, allies are no protection; kinsmen are no protection against the assaults of death.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 288 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> He who looks upon this world with the same eye with which one looks upon a bubble of air, with the same eye with which one looks upon a ray of light, him the king of death (Mara) does not see.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 170 · after Hû, 1878</p>

> He who knows that this body is like a light foam, and that it has the consistency of a ray of light, who has broken the flower-tipped arrows of Mara, — he is able to reach the point where he no longer sees the kingdom of death.

<p class="ref">— Dhammapada 46 · after Hû, 1878. Mara personifies death and temptation.</p>

### Christianity

Before the tomb of his friend Lazarus, Jesus speaks to Martha of the resurrection:

> Jesus said to her: I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me shall live, even though he were dead; and whoever lives and believes in me shall never die. Do you believe this?

<p class="ref">— John 11:25-26 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> Let not your heart be troubled. Believe in God, and believe in me. There are many dwellings in my Father’s house. If it were not so, I would have told you. I am going to prepare a place for you.

<p class="ref">— John 14:1-2 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, and there shall be no more mourning, nor crying, nor pain, for the first things have passed away.

<p class="ref">— Revelation 21:4 · after Segond, 1910</p>

### Islam

The Qur’an recalls that no one escapes death, and places the true reward beyond it:

> Every soul shall taste the drink of death. You shall receive your rewards on the day of resurrection. He who has escaped the fire and who enters paradise, that one shall be blessed, for the life of this world is but a deceptive enjoyment.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 3:185 [Kazimirski III, 182] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

The formula every Muslim pronounces on hearing of a death comes from this verse:

> To those who, when a misfortune strikes them, cry out: We belong to God, and to him we shall return.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 2:156 [Kazimirski II, 151] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

> And as for you, O soul of the faithful! Reassured as to your fate,
>
> Return to God, satisfied with your reward, and pleasing to God;
>
> Enter among the number of my servants;
>
> Enter into my paradise.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 89:27-30 · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Death is certain and **the same for all**: “every soul shall taste death” (Qur’an); neither children nor kinsmen are “a protection against the assaults of death” (Dhammapada). But no tradition leaves it the last word: “he who believes in me shall live, even though he were dead” (Gospel); the Self “is not killed when the body is killed” (Upanishad); “we belong to God, and to him we shall return” (Qur’an); the sage “no longer sees the kingdom of death” (Dhammapada).

#### Where They Differ

Here the paths part most clearly. Christianity and Islam await the **resurrection** and the Judgment, then life with God or far from him; the Hebrew Bible, long reticent about the hereafter, saw the birth of the hope of resurrection that rabbinic Judaism made its own. Hinduism and Buddhism, on the contrary, teach **rebirth**, and set the ultimate goal in escaping this cycle: union with the Absolute for the one, Nirvana for the other. Part Seven takes up these differences.

</div>

## 18. The Experience of the Mystics: The Cloud and the Light

The Scriptures speak of a God who is not seen, of an Absolute that no name can contain, of an “unborn” reached only at the end of the path. In each of the five traditions, some have claimed that something of it can be tasted in this life: we call them the mystics. Their books are not Scriptures, and their own communities sometimes viewed them with suspicion. Yet all of them describe an experience, and though they came from worlds that knew nothing of one another, they often reach for the same images to express it: the cloud, the light, the silence of words.

### Hinduism

Kabir, a weaver of Benares in the 15th century, sang in Hindi for the common people. Hindus hold him to be a saint of devotion (*bhakti*), Muslims claim him as one of their own, and his verses entered the holy book of the Sikhs. He looks for God outside the places where people shut Him away:

> O servant, where dost thou seek Me? Lo! I am beside thee. I am neither in temple nor in mosque: I am neither in Kaaba nor in Kailash: Neither am I in rites and ceremonies, nor in Yoga and renunciation. If thou art a true seeker, thou shalt at once see Me: thou shalt meet Me in a moment of time. Kabîr says, “O Sadhu! God is the breath of all breath.”

<p class="ref">— Kabir, *Songs*, I · tr. Tagore, 1915. Kailash is the sacred mountain of Shiva; the Kaaba, the sanctuary of Mecca.</p>

> If God be within the mosque, then to whom does this world belong? If Ram be within the image which you find upon your pilgrimage, then who is there to know what happens without? Hari is in the East: Allah is in the West. Look within your heart, for there you will find both Karim and Ram; All the men and women of the world are His living forms. Kabîr is the child of Allah and of Ram: He is my Guru, He is my Pir.

<p class="ref">— Kabir, *Songs*, LXIX · tr. Tagore, 1915. Ram and Hari are Hindu names of God, Karim (“the Generous”) a Muslim one; the pir is the spiritual master of the Sufis.</p>

### Judaism

At the end of the 12th century, the first book of the Kabbalah appeared in Provence. Its title, *Sefer HaBahir*, means “the Book of Brightness,” and it opens on the riddle of a light that cannot be seen:

> Rabbi Nehunya ben HaKanah said: One verse says: “And now they do not see the light; it is bright [*bahir*] in the clouds.” Another verse says: “He made darkness His hiding place,” and again: “Cloud and mist surround Him.” Here is a contradiction. A third verse comes and decides between them: “Even darkness is not dark for You; the night shines like the day, darkness is like light.”

<p class="ref">— Sefer HaBahir 1 · working translation from the Hebrew. Verses quoted: Job 37:21; Psalm 18:12; Psalm 97:2; Psalm 139:12.</p>

According to the same book, the light of the first day did not vanish: God set it aside.

> There were two great lights, for it is said: “and there was light,” and it is of both that it is said: “that it was good.” The Holy One, blessed be He, took one and set it aside for the righteous, in the time to come […]. This teaches that the first light, no creature can look upon […].

<p class="ref">— Sefer HaBahir 147 · working translation from the Hebrew</p>

### Buddhism

Shantideva, a monk of the great Buddhist university of Nalanda in the 8th century, left a long poem on the birth and growth of the “thought of Awakening.” He describes its appearance with an image of night and storm:

> Just as a flash of lightning shines for an instant in the darkness of night and clouds, so, by the grace of the Buddha, it happens that the thought of men turns for an instant toward the good.

<p class="ref">— Shantideva, *Bodhicaryavatara* I, 5 · after La Vallée Poussin, 1907</p>

At the end of the path, the meditation on emptiness undoes even the ideas that served to reach it. According to a tradition reported by his translator, Shantideva, having reached this stanza, rose into the air and vanished; his disciples heard only his voice.

> But when one “assumes” the idea of emptiness, when one is steeped in it, the idea of existence disappears; and later, through the habit of this thought that “nothing exists,” the idea of emptiness itself is eliminated. […] And when neither existence nor non-existence any longer presents itself before the mind, then, having no more matter [to affirm or to deny] […], the mind is stilled.

<p class="ref">— Shantideva, *Bodhicaryavatara* IX, 33 and 35 · after La Vallée Poussin, 1907</p>

### Christianity

At the end of the 14th century, in England, an author who remained anonymous wrote *The Cloud of Unknowing* for a young disciple. Between God and the soul, he says, stretches a cloud that thought cannot pierce:

> For of all other creatures and their works, yea, and of the works of God’s self, may a man through grace have fullhead of knowing, and well he can think of them: but of God Himself can no man think. And therefore I would leave all that thing that I can think, and choose to my love that thing that I cannot think. For why; He may well be loved, but not thought. By love may He be gotten and holden; but by thought never. […] And smite upon that thick cloud of unknowing with a sharp dart of longing love; and go not thence for thing that befalleth.

<p class="ref">— *The Cloud of Unknowing*, chap. 6 · ed. Underhill, 1922. “Fullhead”: fullness; “holden”: held.</p>

To pierce this cloud, he advises a prayer reduced to a single word:

> And if thee list have this intent lapped and folden in one word, for thou shouldest have better hold thereupon, take thee but a little word of one syllable: […] for ever the shorter it is the better it accordeth with the work of the Spirit. And such a word is this word GOD or this word LOVE. […] And fasten this word to thine heart, so that it never go thence for thing that befalleth.

<p class="ref">— *The Cloud of Unknowing*, chap. 7 · ed. Underhill, 1922. “If thee list”: if you wish; “lapped and folden”: wrapped and folded.</p>

Two centuries later, in Spain, John of the Cross sums up in a few lines the path of stripping away:

> To taste everything, have a taste for nothing. To know everything, desire to know nothing. To possess everything, wish to possess nothing. To be everything, have the will to be nothing in all things. […] It is in this nakedness that the spirit finds its rest; for, desiring nothing more, it is drawn neither to great things nor to small, because it is at the center of its humility.

<p class="ref">— John of the Cross, *The Ascent of Mount Carmel* I, 13 · after Maillard, 1834 ed.</p>

At the beginning of the 17th century, Jacob Böhme, a shoemaker of Görlitz, places this light in the innermost depth of the soul, and binds it to love:

> […] in the spirit of the soul, in the inner center, is the light of God, which this same soul possesses only so far as it is in the love of God, for this light can be kindled and fanned only by the Holy Spirit.

<p class="ref">— Jacob Böhme, *The Three Principles of the Divine Essence* II, 7 · after Saint-Martin, 1802</p>

### Islam

Ghazali, one of the greatest theologians of Sunni Islam, was teaching in Baghdad when, in 1095, he gave up his chair to follow the way of the Sufis. He tells of this crisis in *Deliverance from Error*. Doubt had first deprived him of all certainty; he says he found his way out by something other than reasoning:

> I owed my cure not to an assemblage of proofs and arguments, but to the light that God caused to enter my heart, a light that illuminates the threshold of all knowledge. Whoever imagines that certainty rests only on arguments diminishes the immense mercy of God.

<p class="ref">— Ghazali, *Deliverance from Error* · after Barbier de Meynard, 1877</p>

He then discovers that the way of the Sufis is not learned from books:

> The goal the Sufis set themselves is this: to tear the soul from the tyrannical yoke of the passions, to deliver it from its guilty inclinations and its evil instincts, so that in the purified heart there may be room only for God and for the invocation of His holy name. […] It was shown to me that its final term could not be revealed by teaching, but only by rapture, ecstasy and the transformation of the moral being. To define health and satiety, to penetrate their causes and conditions, is another thing than to be healthy and satisfied. To define drunkenness […] is another thing than to be drunk.

<p class="ref">— Ghazali, *Deliverance from Error* · after Barbier de Meynard, 1877. The “invocation of the name” is the *dhikr*.</p>

But he at once warns against the words that betray this experience:

> […] they rise successively to degrees that human language cannot make understood, which it could not define without falling into grave and inevitable errors. The degree of nearness they attain is regarded by some as the fusion of being, by others as identification, by others as intimate union. But all these expressions are false […].

<p class="ref">— Ghazali, *Deliverance from Error* · after Barbier de Meynard, 1877</p>

<div class="synthese" markdown="1">

#### Where They Converge

Coming from traditions that, for the most part, knew nothing of one another, the mystics describe the same experience with the same images. The divine hides in the **cloud** and the **darkness**: the light “bright in the clouds” of the Bahir, the “thick cloud of unknowing,” the lightning “in the darkness of night and clouds” of Shantideva. It **escapes thought**: “by thought never” (the Cloud); “to define drunkenness is another thing than to be drunk” (Ghazali); “neither existence nor non-existence” (Shantideva). It does not let itself be shut away in holy places: “neither in temple nor in mosque,” sings Kabir. And everywhere, it is a **light in the heart**: “the light that God caused to enter my heart” (Ghazali), “look within your heart” (Kabir), “the light of God” in the “inner center” of the soul (Böhme). The paths resemble each other too: a **single word** repeated, the word GOD or LOVE of the Cloud, the “invocation of His holy name” of the Sufis; and **stripping away**: “to possess everything, wish to possess nothing” (John of the Cross).

#### Where They Differ

Still, the mystics do not claim to become God. Ghazali rejects the idea outright: those who speak of “fusion,” “identification” or “union” are mistaken. The Jew, the Christian and the Muslim remain creatures before their Creator, even when closest to Him, whereas non-dualist Hinduism speaks of identity with the Absolute. The Buddhist meets no one at all: the mind “is stilled” when even the ideas of existence and non-existence fall away. And the mystics often unsettled their own communities: Kabir mocked the rites of the Brahmins as well as those of the Muslim doctors of the Law; John of the Cross was imprisoned by brothers of his own order; the Kabbalah long remained reserved for a few initiates; and Ghazali devoted a large part of his work to showing that the way of the Sufis does not dispense one from the Law.

</div>

# Part Seven — What Sets Them Apart

<p class="part-intro">The previous chapters have shown real convergences. Stopping there would be dishonest. On essential questions the five traditions disagree, and each holds fast to what makes it unique. Here again, the texts will speak: those in which each tradition affirms what the others do not.</p>

## One God, Several Faces, or No God

This is the most radical difference, and it lies where people often assume the traditions meet.

**Judaism** and **Islam** proclaim a single God, personal, creator, absolutely distinct from his creation, whom no image can represent. The Shema of Israel and the sura of Unity say it with the same force: God is one, he has neither equal nor associate.

**Christianity** also proclaims a single God, but it confesses him in three persons, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. This doctrine of the **Trinity**, formulated at the councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381), is, for Christians, the way the one God has revealed himself. For Judaism, it compromises the unity of God; the Qur’an rejects it explicitly:

> O you who have received the Scriptures! Do not go beyond the right measure in your religion; say of God only what is true. The Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, is the apostle of God and his word, which he cast into Mary; he is a spirit coming from God. Believe then in God and in his apostles, and do not say: There is a trinity. Cease doing so. This will be better for you; for God is one. Glory to him; how could he have a son? […]

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 4:171 [Kazimirski IV, 169] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

**Hinduism** holds together what the others separate. It venerates a multitude of deities (Vishnu, Shiva, the Goddess, Ganesh…), but most of its schools understand them as the faces of a single Reality, **Brahman**. Depending on the school, this Reality is seen as impersonal, beyond all attributes, or as a personal God to be loved and served, such as Krishna in the Bhagavad-Gita. In Hinduism, the worship of images, which the Hebrew Bible and the Qur’an forbid, is a legitimate form of devotion. And the Gita welcomes even the worship of other gods, while subordinating it to its own:

> Even those who, full of faith, worship other deities honor me too, though outside the ancient rule: for it is I who receive and preside over all Sacrifices; but they do not know me in my essence, and they fall once more.

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita IX, 23-24 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

**Buddhism** does not base its path on a creator God. The Buddha refused to speculate on the origin of the world, judging such questions useless for liberation. To a disciple who demanded answers before following the path, he replied with a parable:

> It is as if, Malunkyaputta, a man had been wounded by an arrow thickly smeared with poison, and his friends and companions, his relatives and kinsfolk, were to procure for him a physician or surgeon; and the sick man were to say, ‘I will not have this arrow taken out until I have learnt whether the man who wounded me belonged to the warrior caste, or to the Brahman caste, or to the agricultural caste, or to the menial caste.’ […] That man would die, Malunkyaputta, without ever having learnt this.

<p class="ref">— Majjhima Nikaya 63 (Cula-Malunkya Sutta) · tr. Warren, 1896</p>

Gods exist in Buddhist cosmology, but they are themselves beings subject to birth and death. What Buddhism calls the Ultimate, **Nirvana**, is no one to pray to: it is the extinction of craving and suffering.

So it is inaccurate to say that “all worship the same God.” It is fairer to say that all of them point us toward a reality beyond us, one that judges our lives. But they name it, conceive of it and relate to it in different ways.

## Revelation or Awakening

The five traditions grant their texts different kinds of authority, because they understand their origin differently.

For **Judaism**, the Torah was given by God to Moses; it is the heart of the covenant, and its study is in itself a prayer. For **Islam**, the Qur’an is the uncreated word of God, transmitted word for word in Arabic: a translation is only an interpretation of it, and that is why ritual prayer is performed in Arabic everywhere in the world. For **Christianity**, the supreme revelation is a person before it is a book: Jesus Christ, “the Word made flesh.” The Scriptures are inspired, but written by men, in several languages, and they have been read in translation from the beginning.

For **Hinduism**, the Vedas are not the work of an author, divine or human: they are eternal, and the sages “heard” them. For **Buddhism**, on the other hand, there is no revelation in the proper sense: the Dharma was discovered by a man, through effort and meditation, and the Buddha invites his disciples to accept nothing on his authority alone and to test for themselves the truth of what he teaches.

## One Life or Several

The three religions born of Abraham think of time as a line: each human being lives **a single life**, at the end of which comes the judgment of God. Rabbinic Judaism, Christianity and Islam await, at the end of time, the **resurrection of the dead**, each with its own accents.

The two religions born in India think of time as a circle. **Hinduism** and **Buddhism** teach **samsara**, the cycle of rebirths: the being returns in another existence, human or not, according to the weight of its deeds, its **karma**. The ultimate goal is the **exit from the cycle** rather than eternal life: *moksha*, deliverance, for Hinduism; *Nirvana* for Buddhism.

So when the five traditions affirm with one voice that “each reaps what he has sown,” they set the harvest at different times: on the day of Judgment for some, in a future life for others.

## The Self: Soul, Atman or Non-Self

Judaism, Christianity and Islam teach that man has a **soul** created by God, personal, destined to endure. The Hinduism of the Upanishads goes further: the deep Self, the **atman**, is eternal, and the non-dualist school of Vedanta teaches that it is identical with Brahman, the Absolute itself: “you are That.”

Here Buddhism goes against all the others. It teaches **anatta**, “non-self”: there is, in the human being, no permanent and independent entity, but a stream of phenomena that arise and pass away. Clinging to a “self” is the very root of suffering. This doctrine is one of the hallmarks of Buddhism, and it already set Buddhism apart, in India, from the Brahmanical schools of its time.

## The Paths of Salvation

All the traditions offer a path, but each traces its own.

**Judaism** is above all a way of living within the covenant: the observance of the commandments of the Torah (tradition counts 613 of them), the Sabbath, study, prayer, justice toward one’s neighbor. It does little to seek converts: the “righteous of the nations” who observe the fundamental laws of morality have a share, according to rabbinic tradition (Tosefta, Sanhedrin 13:2), in the world to come.

**Christianity** places **grace** at the center: salvation comes through faith in Jesus Christ, who died and rose again, and not through works alone. Good works are the fruit of this faith, not its price. The Churches long disagreed about how faith and works fit together, but all of them hold Christ to be the one way:

> Jesus said to him: I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.

<p class="ref">— John 14:6 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> There is salvation in no other; for there is under heaven no other name given among men by which we must be saved.

<p class="ref">— Acts 4:12 · after Segond, 1910</p>

**Islam** requires faith in one God and in his Prophet, and the practice of the **five pillars**: the profession of faith, prayer five times a day, the legal alms, the fast of the month of Ramadan and the pilgrimage to Mecca. On the day of Judgment, God weighs deeds, but his mercy surpasses his wrath. And the Qur’an holds Islam to be the religion that God accepts:

> Whoever desires a worship other than resignation to the will of God (*islam*), that worship will not be accepted from him, and in the next world he will be among the wretched.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 3:85 [Kazimirski III, 79] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

**Hinduism** recognizes several paths, which the Bhagavad-Gita brings together: the path of **action** performed without attachment to its fruits (*karma-yoga*), the path of **knowledge** (*jnana-yoga*) and the path of loving **devotion** to God (*bhakti-yoga*). It excludes no path; it brings them all back to its God:

> For as men bow before me, so too do I honor them. All men follow my path, son of Pritha […].

<p class="ref">— Bhagavad-Gita IV, 11 · after Burnouf, 1861</p>

**Buddhism** teaches the **Four Noble Truths**: suffering, its origin in craving, its cessation, and the path that leads there. That path is the **Noble Eightfold Path**: right understanding, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration. No one can be saved by another: “Set to work, then, with ardor. The Tathagatas are content to preach,” says the Dhammapada (v. 276, after Hû). The Tathagatas are the Buddhas.

## Jesus, Bridge and Stumbling Block

No figure shows better what links and what separates the three Abrahamic religions.

For **Christians**, Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God made man, crucified and risen. The Gospel itself asks the question:

> And you, he said to them, who do you say that I am? Simon Peter answered: You are the Christ, the Son of the living God.

<p class="ref">— Matthew 16:15-16 · after Segond, 1910</p>

> And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth; and we beheld his glory, a glory as of the only Son who came from the Father.

<p class="ref">— John 1:14 · after Segond, 1910</p>

For **Judaism**, he is not the awaited Messiah: a Messiah must restore Israel and bring the world the peace that Isaiah announced (“one people shall no longer draw the sword against another people,” Isaiah 2:4, quoted in chapter 5), and the world is not at peace. For **Islam**, Jesus (*'Isa*) is one of the greatest prophets, born of the Virgin Mary by the will of God, a worker of miracles, called “Messiah” and “Word of God” by the Qur’an itself; but he is not the Son of God, and the Qur’an affirms that he was neither killed nor crucified, but raised up by God:

> They say: We put to death the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, the envoy of God. No, they did not kill him, they did not crucify him; a man who resembled him was put in his place […]. They did not really kill him. God raised him up to himself, and God is mighty and wise.

<p class="ref">— Qur’an 4:157-158 [Kazimirski IV, 156] · after Kazimirski, 1869</p>

So the same man is God incarnate for some, a revered prophet for others, and for others still a Jewish teacher of his time. Hinduism has sometimes welcomed him as a sage or as one divine manifestation among others; Buddhism has no doctrine concerning him.

## The Law, Rites and Boundaries

Shared morals should not make us forget that each religion is also a community, with its own rites, calendar and rules.

**Judaism** and **Islam** are religions of the Law: they regulate food (*kashrut*, *halal*), sacred time (the Saturday Sabbath, Friday prayer), ritual purity, family law. **Christianity** gave up early on imposing the ritual law of Moses on non-Jews and has hardly any dietary rules, but it has its sacraments, above all baptism and the Eucharist.

**Hinduism** long tied religious duty, *dharma*, to birth and caste. **Buddhism**, from the start, refused to measure a person by birth: the Buddha welcomed disciples of every condition and taught that nobility comes from deeds, not birth.

The religions also relate differently to other beliefs. Some have sought to be universal and missionary, like Christianity and Islam; others are transmitted first of all by birth, like Judaism and, to a large extent, Hinduism; Buddhism spread throughout Asia by adapting to the cultures it encountered. Their shared history includes centuries of peaceful coexistence, and wars as well. The texts quoted in this book were not always the ones people remembered.

# Conclusion — Different Lamps

<p class="part-intro">An image, often attributed to the Persian poet Rumi, says that the lamps are different, but that the light is the same. What that light is, each tradition says in its own way, and this book does not claim to decide for it.</p>

At the end of this journey, what can we honestly say?

First, that the convergences are no illusion. This book chose its texts, as every anthology does, and it left aside many pages of war or exclusion that also exist in each of the five traditions. But it did not have to twist the ones it quotes: they lie at the heart of each tradition, among its oldest and most widely read texts. Open the Mahabharata, Leviticus, the Dhammapada, the Gospel or the collections of hadiths, and you find the **Golden Rule**: do not do to others what you would not wish to suffer, and wish for them what you wish for yourself. You find the call to **compassion** toward every living being, the duty to **feed the poor** and welcome the stranger, the demand to **speak the truth**, the refusal of theft, the struggle against **anger** and **pride**, the value of **forgiveness**. You find the idea that what counts is the **heart** behind a deed rather than its appearance, and that our deeds follow us. And everywhere you find the same invitation not to cling to what passes away.

Next, that these convergences lie above all on one level: **conduct** and the **inner life**. There the five voices almost overlap. As soon as you reach the ultimate questions (who God is, if he exists; where Scripture comes from; what the soul is; what awaits us after death; how we are saved), the paths part, and each tradition stakes what is most its own. The mystics themselves, so close in their images, part ways when they try to name what they have touched. As Part Seven showed, you cannot make a Buddhist into a monotheist, or a Muslim into a Trinitarian, without betraying both.

None of this is disappointing. It shows that men and women who share neither the same faith, nor the same idea of God, nor the same hope, can still **recognize the same good**. This intuition led the Parliament of the World’s Religions, meeting in Chicago in 1993, to adopt a *Declaration Toward a Global Ethic*, drafted on the initiative of the theologian Hans Küng and founded on the Golden Rule shared by all the traditions. Küng had summed up its spirit a few years earlier: no peace among the nations without peace among the religions, and no peace among the religions without dialogue between them.

There may be a deep reason for this meeting. Each in its own way, these traditions have looked the same human realities in the face: suffering, death, violence, desire that is never satisfied, the temptation to think oneself the center of the world. And all have concluded that serving ourselves brings no peace; peace comes from turning toward something greater than ourselves, whether God, the Absolute or Awakening, and toward our neighbor.

Believers will find their own tradition in these pages, and may see it in a new light as they hear it answer the traditions of others. Readers without faith will at least see that, for three thousand years, much of the best of humanity’s thinking about how to live together has been entrusted to these five great families of texts. And everyone can hold on to one conviction that none of the five contradicts: the light we receive does not diminish when we share it.

## A Sixth Lamp: The Tao Te Ching

<p class="part-intro">This book has kept to five traditions. Ancient China produced another: Taoism, whose founding text, the <em>Tao Te Ching</em> or “Book of the Way and of Virtue,” is attributed to Lao-tzu, a sage of the 6th century BCE according to tradition. A few of its maxims show that this sixth lamp also sheds light on several themes of this book.</p>

**The Ultimate without a name.** Like the Buddha’s “unborn,” or the God of whom *The Cloud of Unknowing* says that “of God Himself can no man think,” the Way escapes words:

> The way that can be expressed in speech is not the eternal Way; the name that can be named is not the eternal Name.

<p class="ref">— Tao Te Ching, chap. I · after Stanislas Julien, 1842</p>

**Rendering good for evil.** The Qur’an, Paul and the Dhammapada taught it in chapter 5; Lao-tzu says it of the Sage:

> He who is virtuous, he treats as a virtuous man; he who is not virtuous, he also treats as a virtuous man. That is the height of virtue.

<p class="ref">— Tao Te Ching, chap. XLIX · after Stanislas Julien, 1842</p>

> He avenges his injuries with benefits.

<p class="ref">— Tao Te Ching, chap. LXIII · after Stanislas Julien, 1842</p>

**The humility of water.** Chapter 12 showed the five traditions promising exaltation to the one who humbles himself; Lao-tzu takes as his model water, which always seeks the lowest place:

> The man of superior virtue is like water. Water excels in doing good to beings and does not strive. It dwells in the places the crowd detests. That is why (the sage) comes close to the Tao. He delights in the humblest position.

<p class="ref">— Tao Te Ching, chap. VIII · after Stanislas Julien, 1842</p>

> I possess three precious things: I hold them and keep them as a treasure. The first is called *affection*; the second is called *economy*; the third is called *humility*, which keeps me from wishing to be the first of the empire.

<p class="ref">— Tao Te Ching, chap. LXVII · after Stanislas Julien, 1842</p>

**Victory over oneself.** The Dhammapada, the Proverbs and the hadith said it in chapter 11:

> He who knows men is prudent. He who knows himself is enlightened. He who subdues men is powerful. He who subdues himself is strong. He who knows how to be content is rich enough.

<p class="ref">— Tao Te Ching, chap. XXXIII · after Stanislas Julien, 1842</p>

**The gift that enriches.** Finally, like the five traditions in chapter 7:

> The Sage does not accumulate (riches). The more he uses (his virtue) in the interest of men, the more it increases. The more he gives to men, the richer he becomes.

<p class="ref">— Tao Te Ching, chap. LXXXI · after Stanislas Julien, 1842</p>

Lao-tzu knows neither a creator God nor revelation: his “Way” is the deep order of the world, which the sage follows without claiming to name it. Yet on the essentials of conduct, his lamp shines with the other five.

## Further Reading

All the translations quoted in this book are in the public domain, and each can be read in full: Burnouf’s *La Bhagavad-Gîtâ* (1861), Fernand Hû’s *Le Dhammapada* (1878), the French Rabbinate Bible (1899-1906), Louis Segond’s Bible (1910), Kazimirski’s *Le Koran* (1869) and Hershon’s *A Talmudic Miscellany* (1880). The mystics of chapter 18 can also be read in full in old editions: Kabir’s *Songs* translated by Tagore (1915), *The Cloud of Unknowing* edited by Evelyn Underhill (1922), the spiritual works of John of the Cross (tr. Maillard; in English, tr. David Lewis, 1864), Böhme’s *Three Principles* (tr. Saint-Martin, 1802; in English, tr. John Sparrow, 1648), Shantideva’s *Bodhicaryavatara* (tr. La Vallée Poussin, 1907; in English, *The Path of Light*, tr. L. D. Barnett, 1909) and Ghazali’s *Deliverance from Error* (tr. Barbier de Meynard, 1877; in English, *The Confessions of Al Ghazzali*, tr. Claud Field, 1909). Readers who prefer to read these Scriptures in English will find them in classic versions that are also in the public domain: the *Sacred Books of the East* edited by F. Max Müller, which include the Upanishads, the Bhagavad-Gita (Telang, 1882), the Dhammapada (Müller, 1881) and the Qur’an (Palmer, 1880); *The Holy Scriptures* of the Jewish Publication Society (1917) for the Hebrew Bible; the King James Version or the World English Bible for the Christian Bible. Readers who wish to go beyond the five traditions can read the whole *Tao Te Ching* of Lao-tzu, from which the appendix quotes a few maxims, in Stanislas Julien’s translation (1842) or, in English, in James Legge’s (1891).
